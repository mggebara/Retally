Methodology Summary: - **Data and sample construction:** The data are monthly Current Population Survey (CPS) interviews from January 1994 to September 2016. Individuals are linked across consecutive months with the Madrian and Lefgren (1999) procedure: matches are made on administrative IDs and confirmed using sex, race and age. The sample starts in 1994 because earlier questionnaires did not allow job-to-job moves to be observed. It keeps civilians over age 16 with non-missing age and education, which gives about 16.9 million observations. Two sets of months are dropped: May to August 1995, which lack the longitudinal link ID, and month pairs that span the eight-month sampling break between interview months 4 and 5.
- **Key variables:** The business-cycle measure is the monthly state unemployment rate (51 units, including DC). Potential experience (PE) is defined as age minus years of education minus 6. "Young" means PE ≤ 10 years and "experienced" means PE > 10 years. The outcome D^hired equals 100 if the person is hired in month t, i.e. either (a) non-employed in t−1 and employed in t, or (b) employed in both months and reports changing firms since last month. New jobs classified as self-employment do not count as hires.
- **Main estimating equation (Eq. 1):** The model is a linear probability model with outcome D^hired_ikst. Its terms are state FE (α_s), month-year FE (δ_t), PE-group indicators D^PE_k, and the interactions D^PE_k × State Unemp. Rate_st. The main effect of the unemployment rate is left out, so each γ_k is the hiring-rate response for PE group k, in percentage points per 1 percentage point of unemployment. The null hypothesis is that all γ_k are equal. The main figures (Fig. 2) use one-year PE bins, and Tables 1 and 2 use the two groups (≤10 vs >10).
- **Preferred specification and controls:** The preferred specification is Table 1, Column 4. It includes state FE, month-year FE and non-parametric demographic FE. The demographic FE are full interactions of gender × race (white/non-white) × Hispanic × education, with seven education categories: <HS, HS, some college, 4-year degree, master's, professional, PhD. Columns 1–3 build up to it: no FE, then state FE, then state + demographic FE. The preferred sample includes all working-age individuals, whatever their labor-force status in t−1. Variants split the sample by origin state in t−1 (employed, unemployed, NILF; Table 2, Fig. 2) and by college degree (Table 9, Fig. 3).
- **Inference and weighting:** Standard errors are clustered at the state level. Regressions are weighted by the average of the CPS sampling weights across the two matched months. Fig. 1 also applies an HP filter to annualized hiring rates to isolate their cyclical component.
- **Composition and labor-supply checks:** Eq. 2 regresses the potential experience of new hires on the state unemployment rate. It includes state, month-year, occupation and industry FE plus demographic FE, with state-clustered SEs and CPS weights. Occupation and industry are crosswalked to consistent 2002 Census codes (508 occupations and 261 industries). Further checks:
  - Table 6 runs state-month-year cell regressions of average PE, and of the ratio of hires' PE to the population's PE, overall and by labor-force status.
  - Table 7 measures search intensity as the number of search methods used (Shimer 2004), for the unemployed only.
  - Table 8 repeats the Table 2 regressions on two restricted samples: unemployed workers available to start within a week, and NILF individuals not in school.
  - Tables 3–4 look at other flows: exits, E→U, job-to-job, U↔NILF, and quits vs. involuntary separations.
- **Wage test (Table 10):** The sample is new hires with non-allocated log weekly wages, deflated to 1994 prices. These wages are observed only in interview months 4 and 8. Log wage is regressed on the state unemployment rate, overall (Panel A) and interacted with the young/experienced split (Panel B). Controls are state FE, month-year FE, detailed industry and occupation FE, and demographic FE, with state-clustered SEs. Columns cover all hires and then hires from employment, unemployment and NILF separately. The test compares the observed pattern with three theories: competitive (equal log-wage cyclicality across groups), classic rigid-wage upgrading (acyclical wages) and bargained cyclical upgrading (young workers' log wages more cyclical).
- **Theory:** The paper builds a stylized single-firm model with random search, endogenous firm size, Stole–Zwiebel bargaining over two worker types, a per-worker fixed cost k and a vacancy cost c. Its key prediction (Proposition 2) is that young workers' log wages are more procyclical than experienced workers' log wages. The empirical work has no train/test split or cross-validation; evaluation rests on coefficient significance and tests that the coefficients are equal across groups.
