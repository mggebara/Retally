Letter Can’t We All Just Get Along? How Women MPs Can Ameliorate
Methodology Summary: - **Data and unit of analysis**: The authors merge an original party-level dataset on women's share of each party's parliamentary delegation (125 parties, 20 Western democracies, 1996–2017) with Comparative Study of Electoral Systems (CSES) surveys covering 81 election-years. Observations are directed party dyads (i, j, t). Each pair enters twice, once as i's partisans rating j and once as j's partisans rating i. The estimation sample is 1,842 directed dyads.
- **Dependent variable**: [party i's supporters' evaluations of out-party j (t)] is the mean 0–10 CSES feeling-thermometer score (0 = strongly dislike, 10 = strongly like) that party i's partisans give out-party j in the survey for election year t. Partisans are respondents who say they are "close" or "a little closer" to party i on the CSES party-identification items. The thermometer is used in its raw 0–10 units, not standardised.
- **Key regressor**: [out-party j's proportion of women MPs (t – 1)] is the share of women in j's parliamentary delegation, lagged one year before the CSES survey (e.g., 2012 composition for the 2013 German election). It runs from 0 (all men) to 1 (all women) and is unstandardised.
- **Controls (full model, Table 1 col. 2)**:
  - [elite right–left distance i, j (t)]: the absolute distance between i's and j's Comparative Manifesto Project left–right (RILE) scores in the current election. The original scale runs from −100 to +100. This variable is standardised, so its coefficient is the effect of a 1-SD change.
  - [i, j are coalition partners (t)]: dummy for governing coalition partners at the time of the survey.
  - [i, j are opposition partners (t)]: dummy for both parties being in opposition.
  - The omitted baseline is a pair with one governing party and one opposition party.
  - Column 1 is a bivariate model with only the women-MPs share plus fixed effects.
- **Specification and inference**: The model is a linear OLS regression with country-year fixed effects. The text says "country-year"; the Table 1 row reads "Country and year fixed effects". Identification therefore comes from within-country, within-election comparisons of out-parties. Standard errors are robust and clustered by election (CSES election study). Models are judged by coefficient sign and significance with two-tailed tests (p < 0.05, p < 0.01), and adjusted R² is reported.
- **Sample restrictions**: Only dyads in which both parties i and j had at least four MPs in the year before the election survey are kept. Smaller parties are dropped, and Appendix S2 adds them back as a robustness check. The sample covers 20 Western countries and CSES elections from 1996 to 2017.
- **Subgroup models**: Table 1 columns 3 and 4 re-estimate the full model with dyad-mean ratings built only from self-identified women partisans (col. 3, N = 1,836) and only from men partisans (col. 4, N = 1,833).
- **Robustness checks (appendix)**:
  - Controls for out-party family (S3).
  - Earlier vs. later periods (S4).
  - In-party women's share (S5).
  - Electoral-system proportionality (S6).
  - Nonlinearity past parity (S7).
  - Out-parties led by women vs. men (S8).
  - Country rather than country-year fixed effects, and other fixed-effects setups (S9–S10).
  - A stacked individual-level dataset with individual fixed effects, one row per respondent per out-party rated (S11).
  - There is no train/test split or cross-validation.
