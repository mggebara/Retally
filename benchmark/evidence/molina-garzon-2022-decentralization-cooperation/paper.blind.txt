Abstract: Collective action among public officials is necessar y for the effective delivery of many s ocial services, but relatively
little is known about how it can be fostered through policy reforms. In this article, we compare cooperation among public
officials within decentralized versus centrally-administered municipalities in Honduras. Leverag ing a quasi-experiment in
health sector reform, coupled with behavioral games and social network surveys, we find that decentralization is associated
with greater cooperation. When they are able to communicate, health sector wor kers in decentralized municipalities con-
tribute more to a public good than their centrally-administered counterparts. This increase in cooperative behavior results
in part from the decentralization reform engendering greater numbers of interactions and stronger ties across different levels
of government. These findings indicate that institutional reforms like decentralization can favorably reconfigure patterns of
social interactions across public organizations, which is an important component of administrative capacity in developing
countries.

At the same time, social dilemmas that are prevalent
in many public service delivery systems, like the health
sector context examined here, make it difficult to a chie ve
this kind of cooperation and joint action (Olson 1965;
Ostrom 2007). For example, consider a busy waiting
room full of patients in a rural health center. Each doctor
and nurse confront a tension between their individual in-
terests and those of the group. Each individual provider
may prefer to take his or her time and attend a smaller
number of patients wh ile expec ting that others will cover
the overflow, whereas the group together could provide
care to the high volume of patients if all the providers
cooperated by seeing a couple of additional cases over
the course of the workday. Beyond this simple example,
similar and even more severe tensions between individ-
ual and group interests exist throughout health systems:
from staff within a single health center, to health centers
in a regional network, and up to manag ing organizations
and their state-level regulators. Furthermore, these social
dilemmas can be exacerbated by resource scarcity and
the prevalence of public health crises that often charac-
terize health systems in less developed countries. As a
result, they work to reinforce barriers to cooperation,
ultimately producing ineffect ive and unresponsive health
care systems unable to serve their populations.
A key challenge, then, is to induce public officials to
cooperate in the pursuit of enhanced social welfare. The
degree to which a governance system is able to overcome
such social dilemmas will ultimately influence the qual-
ity, equity, and cost-effectiveness of public services within
that system. Cooperation among representatives operat-
ing at different government levels is needed in order to
design interventions that match policy responses to local
circumstances (Blomkamp 2018; Ham 1937), mobilize
resources to f und the interventions (Johnson 2016), and
leverage economies of scale in the implementation of re-
sponses (Feiock and Lee2010; Waddell and Brown 1997).
Recognizing the many challenges to fostering ef-
fective and responsive public service provision systems,
national governments and international organizations
have promoted a broad range of institutional reforms in
the public sector, aiming to increase capacity for effective
public service delivery (World Bank 2000). Decentral-
ization is among the most prominent of such reforms
(Faguet 2014). The defining feature of decentralization
is the transfer of governance rights, resources, and re-
sponsibilities from the central government to more local
jurisdictions and administrative units (Faguet 2014;
Schneider 2003). There is now an extensive body of liter-
ature on the purported advantages and disadvantages of
decentralization, including improved information and
accountability, on the one hand (Diamond and Tsalik
1999; Hayek 1945; Oates 1972), and possibilities for
elite capture and the perpetuation of inequalities, on the
other (Agrawal and Ribot 1999; Crook and Manor 2000;
Persha and Andersson 2014). However, there remains
considerable uncertainty as to how decentralization
affects the prospects for cooperation on the frontlines
of service delivery. Despite the recognized importance
of bureaucratic discretion and the prevalence of public
sector reforms across the developing world, few studies
have examined how institutional reforms, and the gov-
ernance structures they usher in, influence cooperation
and capacity for collective ac tion among civil servants at
the subnational level.

Decentralization and Cooperation
among Bureaucrats
All governance regimes face the challenge of delivering
public services that respond to local needs in a cost-
effective manner (Andersson 2002). This, in turn, re-
quires governance systems to organize themselves to in-
corporate context-appropriate knowledge and informa-
tion into the delivery of locally tailored public services,
and to administer the production of public services in
ways that can take advantage of economies of scale where
possible (Oakerson 1999). Accomplishing this dual ob-
jective requires considerable coordination and coopera-
tion between multiple actors operating at different levels

and across different organizations within the governance
system. Decentralized and centralized regimes have
pursued different strategies to achieve these cooperative
dynamics. At a very basic level, centralized regimes seek
to ensure cross-level cooperation “in house” by organiz-
ing public service decisions in large-scale, public, and hi-
erarchical structures controlled through decisions at the
top of the hier archy. Decentralized regimes, on the other
hand, tend to rely more on contributions from a diverse
set of relatively autonomous public and private organi-
zations and incorporate a wider variety of accountability
mechanisms, such as local elections or performance-
based contracts, to promote cross-level cooperation.
While decentralization reforms differ greatly in
implementation and results (see the supporting infor-
mation[SI], p. 3, for a more thorough discussion), the
common and most prominent feature of decentral-
ization is the transfer of governance rights, resources,
and responsibilities away from the central government
(Bossert 1998; Faguet 2004, 2014; Rondinelli et al. 1989;
Schneider 2003).
Decentralization can lead to improvements in
governance, responsiveness to local needs, increased
knowledge of local problems, electoral accountability,
and greater flexibility for experimentation in the devel-
opment of solutions, among other benefits. However,
it can sometimes end badly due to elite capture to the
detriment of the poor, increased transaction costs, and
greater complexity in the governance system due to the
multiplicity of actors and interests. The extent to which
the potential benefits of decentralization materialize, and
its risks are reduced or avoided, depends in large part on
how the reformed governance system is able to create and
strengthen accountability mechanisms (Andersson 2006;
Cohen and Peterson 1999; Diamond and Tsalik 1999;
Hayek 1945; Oates 1972; Ostrom, Gardner, and Walker
1994; Ribot 2002). Greater upward accountability (e.g.,
national governments hold local governments and other
administrative units to account for achieving policy
objectives cost-effectively) and downward accountability
(e.g., citizens hold local governments and other admin-
istrative units accountable for addressing their concerns)
can, in turn, bring about increased motivation for local
governance actors from different organizations to inter-
act more regularly and frequently, which enhances the
prospects for interorganizational cooperation.
Public servants and frontline service producers face
a classic social dilemma. Cooperation by all actors can
achieve b etter collective outcomes in the public sector,
but individuals face an incentive to free-ride, especially
where their efforts are not easily monitored by actors
at other levels of the hierarchy, as is often the case for
street-level bureaucrats. Social dilemma games have been
used for decades as a measure of cooperation (Dawes
et al. 1986; Sally 1995; Swensson 1967). The single
most consistent finding from this body of literature is
that face-to-face communication enhances cooperation
(Ledyard 1995; Ostrom, Gardner, and Walker 1994;
Sally 1995), suggesting that those who interact more fre-
quently in person are likely to behave more cooperatively
toward each other in regard to a collective goal. Simply
knowing the identity of other participants increases
cooperation by situating an interaction within a social
environment and decreasing social distance (Bohnet and
Frey 1999a, 1999b). Group composition affects coopera-
tion, as players are influenced by their prior experiences
and existing relationships (Cardenas 2004; Cardenas and
Ostrom 2004). All of this suggests that if an institutional
reform increases the frequency of interactions, it has the
potential to decrease social distance and increase levels
of cooperation.
We theorize that decentralization has the potential
to strengthen cooperation between actors within local
governance systems. Whether this potential is realized,
we argue, will ultimately depend on three intermediate
processes often associated with decentralization: (1)
increased number of opportunities for local governance
actors to interact regularly; (2) increased motivations,
through more autonomy or accountability, for those in-
teractions to take place; and (3) more face-to-face com-
munication between local governance actors. Although
the achievementof these elements could lead to increased
cooperation, cooperation by itself may not necessarily
lead to improvements in the delivery of public services
and associated health outcomes, which likely require sev-
eral other inputs (e.g., a funded mandate, reliable infra s-
tructure, and well-trained personnel). However, cooper-
ation between governance actors represents an important
component to the process and, thus, is worthy of study.
In the health sector context, numerous scholars have
emphasized the importance of complex accountability
relationships among principals and agents in shaping
incentives, positively or negatively, for delivering services
(Bossert 1998; Brinkerhoff 2004). Responding to the
perception of inadequate or suboptimal incentive struc-
tures, health sector decentralization attempts to change
institutional arrangements of accountability across ju-
risdictions or functions of service delivery. Cross-level
monitoring—where

governance structures, particularly those that include

Motivations for greater interactions could also occur

to targeted individuals (Mettler 2002; Pierson 1993).
Compared to civil servants in centralized systems, civil

autonomy will motivate workers to take greater initiative

As civil servants, public officials, and administra-
tors interac t more under decentralized regimes, these
interactions begin to engender greater familiarity, ease
communication barriers, and facilitate both trust and
social learning (Faguet, Fox, and Pöschl 2015; Roling and
Jiggins 1998; Sally 2001; Wollenberg 2001). We argue that
these repeated cross-level interactions lead to behavioral
changes. Namely, civil servants and public officials under
decentralization will cooperate more w ith one another.
We assess this proposition using data from actual civil
servants and public officials working to deliver primary
care health services in Honduras.
Health Sector Reform in Honduras
As in many developing countries, the primary health care
service provider in Honduras is the public sector through
its Ministry of Health (MOH), which covers about 60%
of the total population and the vast majority of indi-
viduals in rural areas (Secretaria de Salud Honduras
et al. 2013). In line with the global popularity of de-
centralization (e.g., Bossert 2014 200), the MOH began
experimenting with the reform around 2005–6 and
has been implementing its current decentralized health
services deliver y model since about 2007. At the time of
this study, health sector decentralization was in place in
over 90 of the 298 municipalities in Honduras (Zarychta
et al. 2019a). In addition to structural changes, namely,
devolution from the central MOH to its regional health
authorities and deconcentration to intermediary or-
ganizations via performance-based agreements, this
reform also attempted to shift pr imary health care from
a curative approach to a greater emphasis on prevention.
That said, by far the most notable change under the
reform is the introduction and empowerment of local
intermediar y organizations within the service delivery
chain, as shown in the right panel of Figure 1. 1 These
decentralized managing organizations have the role of
administering local health service provision in terms of
personnel, supplies, and activities, as well as supporting
and ensuring the achievement of specified performance
indicators relate d to the production and uptake of health
services (MOH 2009). Overall, this reform reflects the
“complexities of multiple, semi-autonomous actors . .
. jointly producing services in a context where the state
ultimately retains primary responsibility” (Zarychta
et al. 2019b, 5). In terms of both its substantive features
and its staggered rollout over time, the health sector
decentralization in Honduras is an excellent case for
assessing whether and how institutional reforms may be
able to engender cooper ation among public officials and
street-level bureaucrats.
Experimental Design, Data, and
Methods
We utilize a quasi-experimental research design coupled
with data from a public goods game (PGG) and social
network sur vey to assess how decentralization influences
cooperation among public servants charged with de-
livering local health services in Honduras. Specifically,
we conducted a daylong workshop with administr ators,
doctors, nurses, social workers, and municipal officials
in 31 municipalities, 21 of which were decentralized and
10 of which were a matched set of comparable, centrally-
administered municipalities. The municipalities selected
for this study are shown in Figure 2. They were identified
principally based on their likelihood of being targeted for
the reform, while also ensuring geographic dispersion
across the country (following the methods in Zar ychta
et al. 2019a, 2019b). Additionally, we included no more
than three municipalities from a single state, due to the
limited number of regional-level health administrators
working in each state. A more detailed description of the
selection strategy is in the supporting information (p. 3).

Variables
The main outcome variable for this study is cooperation
as reflected by individual contributions in the PGG, a
classic social dilemma game that follows the experimen-
tal design of Isaac and Walker (1988). In the game, each
participant was assigned 10 lempiras6 at the beginning
of each round and given the opportunity to decide how
much to contribute to the group fund and how much to
keep. All decisions were made privately. The total sent
to the g roup fund was doubled and then divided evenly
among all participants; the total earn ings for a single
player in each round were equal to the amount saved in
his or her private fund, plus his or her portion of t he
amount redistributed from the group fund. Participants
were instructed that the payoffs they received depended
upon their own investment as well as the group’s. The
players faced the same decision during 10 consecutive
rounds; there was no communication during the first
five rounds, whereas in the second five they were allowed
to communicate before making their decisions.
Individual contributions to the group fund are a
measure of cooperation toward the common good, with
higher contributions reflecting greater cooperation. We
designed the game so that the game’s social optimum is
achieved when all participants send their entire endow-
ment to the group fund in each round. Everyone in the
group was given the same information about the game’s
design and received updates on the total contribution to
the group fund at the end of each round so they could
adjust their individual decision strategies as they saw fit.
Overall, 225 administrators, doctors, nurses, social
workers, and municipal officials across 31 municipalities
made decisions about whether and how much to con-
tribute to a group fund in each of the 10 rounds of this
PGG, for a total of 2,250 observations. For context, one
of the most common participant profiles in our sample
was a female nurse in her late thir ties with a high school
a very few cases where designated individuals could not attend the

Analysis and Results
We used weighted regression analysis to assess the rela-
tionship between decentr alization and the level of coop-
eration exhibited by public servants. The main indepen-
dent variable in this analysis is the administration type in
the municipality, namely, a binary indicator of whether it
was decentralized (1) or centrally-administered (0). This
section describes our main results relating to coopera-
tion as well as a secondary analysis examining reported
cross-level ties among participants to assess one poten-
tial mechanism by which decentralization may be able to
increase cooperation.
To analyze the game results, we compare contri-
butions made by individuals in each setting within the
game’s 10 rounds. We also compare contribution behav-
ior in the two regimes between types of participants (R,
AI, HC, or M) in order to account for the presence of
a differential effect of the reform for particular agents
within each regime. We use the contribution level during
each round of the game (0–10 lempiras) as the depen-
dent variable. The results present a linear estimation
(OLS) accounting for the clustered characteristics of
the observations. For a robustness check, we include
a multilevel Tobit model to account for the censured
characteristic of contribution level and g rouped variance
by individual and municipality levels (see SI Appendix F,
Table SA3).
The control variables include individual demo-
graphic characteristics, years working in the sector,
participants in the session, known individuals in the
room, an indicator of rounds in the game, communica-
tion when making decisions, total group contribution in
previous rounds, and dummyvariables for type of player.
Table 1 presents the mean differences of the main char-
acteristics of the participants in the workshop by type of
administration, weighted based on the municipal-level
propensity scores. We see that bureaucrats in decen-
tralized municipalities tend to be younger, have spent
fewer years in the health sec tor and in their current jobs,
and represent a higher proportion of doctors and social
workers compared to centr ally-administered municipal-
ities. We do not consider this to be a function of our
sampling, but rather a reflection of the expected changes
accompanying the decentralization reform, namely,
substantive differences in staff s election due to the higher
discretion allowed under the reform (Zar ychta et al.,
2019b, 2020). We include control variables accounting
for these characteristics in the analyses that follow.

Cross-Leve l Interactions as a Potential
Mechanism Linking Decentralizat ion to
Cooperation
To better understand a potential mechanism by which
the decentralization reform in Honduras promoted
higher cooperation levels among public servants in the
health system, we explored the frequency of reported
cross-level t ies among the participants. For this analysis,
we use data from the post-survey where we asked work-
shop participants to characterize their relationships with
all other participants attending that day. Specifically,
we asked individuals to report whether they knew each
other person prior to the workshop, the basis of their re-
lationship,7 and the number of hours they spent together
during the previous week. We used the responses to
these prompts to measure the numbers of cross-level ties
that each individual realized with the other workshop
participants. These data provide an indication of the
structure of relationships among public servants in that
we recruited individuals from all lynchpin roles that
constitute the local health system in each municipality
under both administrative struc tures as described earlier,
but they are limited in that they are representations of
those governance systems rather than the systems in their
entirety. Bearing this limitation in mind, we nonetheless
think that the subsequent exploratory analysis highlights
an important and under-emphasized mechanism in both
the decentralization and public goods literatures: rela-
tionships among civil servants at different administrative
levels.
Accordingly, we focus this analysis on cross-level
ties, the connections between public servants at different
levels within the health service delivery hierarchy, as re-
flected in Figure 2. In terms of measurement, we identify
the total possible number of cross-level ties a participant
could have realized prior to arriving at our workshop vis-
à-vis the other participants present there, and then count
up the number of t ies they actually reported with those
individuals and calculate the proportion of cross-level
ties realized.8 We utilize two versions of this measure
as the outcome variables in our subsequent analyses: a
weak version where ties are based on knowing the other
participant as a colleague and a strong version where the
tie is based on friendship with that other participant (see
the SI, p. 11, for additional details on the construc tion of
these variables).

Discussion
Our results highligh t important understudied effects of
institutional reforms like decentralized health service
delivery: such reforms can increase cooperation among
civil servants, and in the Honduran case this appears
to be on account of greater motivations, both financial
and psychological, for actors across different levels of the

increase in regular, cross-level interactions leads to more
face-to-face communication, which ultimately promotes
the kinds of joint actions needed for achieving collective
goals in the public sector. Importantly, at least part of
the decentralization–cooperation relationship appears to
operate through behavioral mechanisms, such as an in-
creased sense of familiarity, an easing of communication
barriers, a greater sense of belonging, and social learning
processes that come along with regular and repeated
interactions among civil servants.
In a decentralization reform like the one imple-
mented in Honduras, the addition of an intermediary
agent within the system may not always be expected to
translate to a higher degree of cooperation among public
servants. For example, shifting social service produc-
tion from in-house management systems to external,
relatively more autonomous agents could potentially
increase transaction costs or disrupt the previous work
dynamics. On the other hand, one might suppose that
creating a new administrative level under the decen-
tralization reform would almost necessarily increase
cross-level ties. Our evidence, both in the analysis pre-
sented here and robustness checks in the supporting
information, indicates that the reform does genuinely
promote an increased frequency of interactions within
the system beyond what would be expected from just in-
troducing a new level. Furthermore, these additional ties
do appear to tr anslate to higher cooperation irrespective
of associated increases in transaction costs, which may
be in part a reflection of the relatively low amounts
of interaction in these rural health systems before and
absent the reform (Zarychta 2020).
Communication consistently increases cooperation
in social dilemma games (Ledyard 1995; Ostrom, Gard-
ner, and Walker 1994; Sally 1995), but its effect varies
based on group composition (Cardenas 2004) and is
most effective when explicitly activating social norms
(Lopez and Villamayor-Tomas 2017). Communication
itself serves as a coordination game that signals relation-
ships between players (Sally 2005). Participants in our
PGG knew each other but had never interacted in the
specific game context before. During the communication
rounds, participants are able to make promises and com-
mitments to each other, helping to fill gaps in knowledge
about the intentions of others (Bochet and Putterman
2009; Bornstein 1992; Kerr and Kaufman-Gilliland
1994). Since these promises are not binding, however,
their effectiveness is likely to be moderated by the extent
to which participants v iew each other as trustworthy.
Differences in group-level dynamics thus moderate the
effect of communication on cooperation. Consistent
with this, we see a difference in contribution levels
between the decentralized and centrally-administered
settings only when communication is allowed.
Moreover, we believe this could be related to the
increased frequency of cross-level interactions among
members of the health system in decentralized mu-
nicipalities compared to centrally-administered ones.
Several particular aspects of health sector decentral-
ization reform in Honduras a re at play here, i ncluding
a delegation of oversight responsibilities from the na-
tional le vel to regional health authorities, as well as
the further transfer of functions to local government
and
non-governmental
organizations.
Additionally,
as part of the reform, an agreement with a local ad-
ministrative intermediary organization is established,
transferring funds to this organization in exchange for
performing the role of administering the network of
health centers under supervision of the corresponding
regional health authority. Resource transfers are based
on local characteristi cs, as well as achievement of sector
targets. This last condition creates incentives for more
frequent interactions, thus helping to promote coop-
eration between local organizations as an attempt to

through increased frequency of communication among
members of the health system that familiarity and social

Even though the Honduran reform was primarily
an administrative decentralization change, there is also
a change in fiscal incentives. The promise of resource
transfers based on results increases the pressure to
perform. In order to reach established goals, local orga-
nizations are incentivized to join forces and seek external
supports or coproduce local services. The reform gener-
ates an additional push to interact and be accountable at
different levels of authority in order to ensure sustainable
access to transfers, as well as to join efforts to seek addi-
tional funds to guarantee financial capacity to maintain
services. This incentivized collaboration may increase
the frequency of cross-level interactions even if the
promise of financial transferences does not materialize
in the short term, thus resulting in higher coopera-
tion. Following Axelrod’s (1984) origin of cooperation
explanation, decentralization reform had a positive
effect on the group’s willingness to act collectively be-
cause “they value future payoffs and expect to interact
again an indefinite number of times” (in Boix and
Posner 1998).
Overall, our findings support the idea that decen-
tralization created an institutional setting that facilitated
and incentivized the creation of increased communica-
tion and cross-level ties. Those cross-level ties contain
relational capital that may be utilized by members of the
service delivery system, thus increasing their propensity
to cooperate. Consistent with Shami (2012), our analysis
shows that stronger hierarchical connections improve
collective action outcomes by allowing for a better flow
of information between individuals at various levels,
which “oils the wheels of decision making” (Adger 2003,
389) and strengthens the relational dynamics among
members of the health system. Furthermore, it is also
possible that the higher levels of cooperation we observe
among local civ il servants in decentralized settings could
translate to a higher reservoir for social capital. Social
capital, one should note, is not always associated with
positive outcomes—the literature offers several exam-
ples of “negative social capital” effects such as negative
outgroup externalities, negative emotions, or rivalries
(Portes 1998; Woolcock and Narayan 2000). For the
purpose of public service provision, we believe that more
cooperative dynamics among the members of the health
system at the municipal level could translate to stronger
social capital, which may facilitate a better provision of
public services. However, further evidence is needed to
support such a theory.
Although we cannot directly test the relationship
between our measure of cooperation and service pro-
vision with the present data set, we created a measure
of perceived health system performance using responses
from a separate, nationwide survey that asked health
workers to estimate how much and in what direction
health outcomes in the municipalit y changed over 5
years. Consistent with our expectation, we found a
positive correlation between perceived improvements
in health and group cooperation, measured by the
average contribution in the public goods game (pair-
wise correlation of [withheld]7, p = .04 for the whole sample,
and [withheld]9, p = .07 considering only the decentralized
municipalities); see Table SA16 in the supporting infor-
mation for more details. This provides some preliminary
support for increased cooperation among public of-
ficials leading to better service provision, and it is in
line with Zarychta (2020), and Root et al. (2020) who
find positive health service effects of decentralization in
Honduras.
It is important to consider, however, that there are
limitations on the conclusions we c an reach due to our
moderate sample size in terms of the number of mu-
nicipalities we were able to consider for our workshops.
This was an unavoidable limitation due to the logistical
restrictions associated with the advance of the reform
process in the country and the hierarchical structure
of our sample, which limited the type of participants
who could attend our workshop without affecting local
health system operations. At the same time, we also had
to ensure we included representatives of all key roles
within the local health system under both administrative
structures. Because of this, it is p ossible that some of
our analyses are somewhat underpowered, but to our
knowledge this is the first study assessing the effect of de-
centralization on cooper ation among public servants, so
we cannot compare our results to determine the positive
predictive value (i.e., probability that a research claim is
true; Ioannidis 2005). Nevertheless, we acknowledge the
limitation of this study and recommend caution using
these results for policy implementation. Further research
with larger samples is needed in this area.

presented here indicates that groups from decentral-
ized municipalities cooperate more a nd have a higher
frequency of strong connections between civil servants,
when compared to centrally-administered municipal-
ities. We argue that an institutional reform, such as
decentralization of the Honduran health sector, can cre-
ate a setting that facilitates and incentivizes the creation
of greater numbers of cross-level ties, and thus leads to
increases in cooperation among civil servants.
Furthermore, we present evidence of the potential
role of public policy in promoting cooperation among
civil servants, addressing a lack of understanding about
the mechanisms by which decentralization may be able
to improve governance dynamics. Because of the mixed
evidence on the effects of decentralization, we think
there is a strong need to focus on unpacking the mech-
anisms by which such an institutional reform modifies
work connections and relational capital to change the
way public services are delivered. Specifically, we argue
that by changing the institutional structure through
adding intermediary managing organizations to the
system and incorporating incentives associated with the
achievement of outcomes, this decentralization reform
promotes an increase in the number of cross-level con-
nections, frequency of interactions, and communication,
which ultimately improves cooperation. This result
supports the more general idea that cooperation can be
induced by providing new opportunities for repeated in-
teractions among individuals (Cardenas 2003; El lickson
1991; Ostrom 1990, 2007; Smith 1982).
Finally, we suggest that it is important to consider
linking connections of agents across administrative
levels of the public sector (i.e., cross-level ties) when
analyzing t he effec tiveness of institutional reforms like
decentralization. For example, one application for these
changes in cross-level cooperation dynamics is an effec t
on bridging social capital as it relates to connections
between indiv iduals across groups (Szreter and Wool-
cock 2004). Linking social capital is a particular form
of bridging social capital, as it refers to relationships
between individuals in different positions within a hi-
erarchical power structure, including connections of
individuals to formal institutions (Barr 1998; Kozel and
Parker 2000; Mayoux 2001; Narayan 1999; Woolcock
2001). Linking social capital effects among civil ser-
vants and public employees are often overlooked when
studying institutional reforms like decentralization, yet
it may be decisive for understanding the implementation
and outcomes of such reforms. Increased interactions
between bureaucrats may facilitate collective action by
public employees in their role as providers of public
services, but whether the increased cooperation will
translate into more effective service delivery and better
administrative capacity is likely to depend on several
other conditional factors, such as the availability of
financial and human resources, as well as proper health
infrastructure. These are important future avenues for
research that explores the potential effects of decentral-
ization reforms on different types of social capital and
the degree to which they lead to better public service
provision.
