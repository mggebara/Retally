MAJOR REFORMS IN ELECTRICITY PRICING: EVIDENCE FROM A 
QUASI-EXPERIMENT 
 
Xavier Labandeira, José M. Labeaga, Jordi J. Teixidó* 
August 5, 2021 
 
The global energy mix and cost structure of the power industry are experiencing a 
redefinition. Many countries are revamping electricity-pricing systems to guarantee 
fixed-cost recovery, often by raising the fixed charge of two-part tariff schemes. 
However, a key assumption of two-part tariff schemes and associated fixed cost 
recoveries is that consumers discriminate fixed from marginal costs. We conduct a 
quasi-experiment with data from a major electricity price reform recently implemented 
in Spain and find robust evidence indicating that consumers fail to distinguish between 
fixed and marginal costs. As a result, policymakers are not achieving the goal of cost 
recovery (JEL: C99, D12, L11, L94, L98, Q41 and Q48). 
Keywords: fixed-cost recovery, electricity demand, quasi-experiment, two-part tariff

According to economic theory, a two-part tariff (TPT) is a pricing scheme which, by means 
of a fixed charge, guarantees the recovery of fixed costs by natural monopolies; moreover, it 
ensures allocative efficiency if the per-unit price equals the marginal cost (Coase 1946). 
Importantly, because fixed charges are independent of the quantity consumed, consumers 
have no incentive to change their consumption quantities in response to a fixed charge. This 
allows the industry to raise revenues to cover fixed costs and minimizes deadweight losses. 
However, if consumers fail to separate fixed charges from marginal prices in their 
consumption decisions, the price signals sent may well be distorting in the short- and long- 
run, jeopardizing cost recovery, investment decisions and allocative efficiency. The main 
objective of this study is to empirically test how changes within TPT schemes may differ 
from the predictions of economic theory.  
This is especially relevant for the electricity industry, which has traditionally operated a TPT 
in most countries and faces a major investment cycle, given the need to decarbonize the sector 
to confront the climate crisis.1 Fixed charges in electricity bills have been low in general and 
markups on the volumetric price component have largely covered fixed costs. However, the 
transition to a low-carbon power sector is triggering a shift in the cost structure, with the 
intensification of capital costs and a decline in variable costs because of an expanded 
renewable energy supply (Bushnell and Novan 2018; Würzburg et al. 2013; Sensfuß et al. 
2008). As a result, current electricity pricing systems are proving inadequate to warrant the 
fixed-cost recovery (European Commission 2015; Baatz 2017) and are at considerable risk 
with the rapid emergence of more energy-efficient appliances, which further reduce 
electricity consumption and hence the sector’s revenues. Moreover, in this context of energy 
transition where short-run marginal costs can vary dramatically, price signals reflecting the 
actual marginal costs of electricity production are key to enhance market efficiency (Burger

In this paper we use a quasi-experimental design based on the major 2013 electricity price 
reform in Spain, which consisted of a substantial increase in the fixed charge within the 
residential electricity bill, combined with a reduction of the marginal price. The main aim of 
the reform was to raise industry revenues to improve the financial sustainability of the 
electricity system (Orden IET/1491/2013).3 By means of a demand model, we empirically 
estimate household responses to this price reform and evaluate how it affects revenues raised 
by the power sector. We also assess the distributional effects of the implemented changes. 
The aforementioned reform brought about a 112% increase in the fixed charge with a non-
revenue neutral reduction in the regulated volumetric part of 35%. Wholesale electricity 
prices remained relatively constant (see Figure A1 in the Appendix). Altogether, the changes 
resulted in an increased average cost per kWh. Our identification strategy involves exploiting 
the data stratification method adopted by the Spanish Household Budget Survey (HBS). In 
particular, the Spanish HBS is stratified into 26 annual subsamples identical in all observable 
characteristics by definition except the dates on which the households were interviewed. We 
exploit the exogeneity of the date of interview of households and the sampling design strategy 
of the Spanish Statistical Office (INE) to divide households into comparable control and 
treated groups and apply a difference-in-difference approach. We control for potential 
confounders, so that being interviewed before or after the reform approximates a random 
allocation of the reform within a standard experimental setting.

in response to the reform. This reduction in consumption is not consistent with a response to

this empirical finding provides compelling evidence that consumers do not distinguish fixed 
from marginal costs, in contrast to what economic theory assumes. Thus, the welfare gains 
derived from TPT schemes are lower than initially expected. As discussed by Borenstein and 
Bushnell (2019), in the same way that low prices may encourage wasteful use (i.e. prices set 
below the social marginal cost), excessively high prices can act as a barrier to further 
electrification of energy and transportation services. Indeed, because of the deadweight loss 
caused by the misperception of the marginal price, our results seem to be consistent with the 
latter hypothesis. 
Secondly, we observe about a 12% reduction in household electricity expenditure, meaning 
that the revenue increase from the higher fixed charge was lower than the revenue loss from 
the resulting contraction in demand or, alternatively, from induced reductions in contracted 
power, which, nonetheless, do not seem to be the main driver behind this change. Hence, 
rather than improving the fixed-cost recovery, a rise in the fixed charge might tend to push 
electric utilities towards a “death spiral” (Costello and Hemphill 2014) and do little to 
guarantee their financial sustainability. This has far-reaching, yet also timely policy 
implications for the power sector. More generally, our results show that the fixed cost 
recovery via fixed charges critically depends on the ability of consumers to distinguish fixed 
costs from marginal costs.  
We also show by quantile regression that, while households with the highest level of 
electricity consumption reduced their demand notably, households exhibiting the lowest level 
registered much smaller decreases (even non-significantly different from zero at the bottom 
of the distribution). We argue that this pattern among the lowest electricity consumers comes 
about not because of an alignment between their behavior and theory predictions, but rather 
because of their limited capacity to further diminish electricity consumption as they approach 
the household basic minimum level. In terms of income distribution, progressivity indices 
confirm that the reform was indeed regressive, as expected from fixed charge increases in 
energy goods (Burger et al. 2020).  
The paper contributes to the sizeable literature on the consumer response to price signals. 
Recent studies have shown the extent to which households respond to marginal prices in non-
linear price schedules, usually in the context of increasing block pricing (Ito 2014; Khan and 


 
5 
Wolak 2013; Nataraj and Hanemann 2011; Borenstein 2009). In the case of electricity, given 
the information cost of understanding non-linear pricing structures, there is evidence that 
consumers tend to respond to average prices rather than to marginal prices. Importantly, their 
degree of energy literacy affects such non-optimizing behaviors (Blasch et al. 2017; Jessoe 
and Rapson 2014; Wolak 2011). However, research to date has not determined how 
households might respond to changes in electricity prices affecting the fixed component. In 
contrast to changes in pricing blocks, the fixed component is totally independent of the kWh 
consumed and, more importantly, seeks cost recovery. 
Ex-ante evidence shows that electricity consumption increases if the rise in fixed charge is 
revenue-neutral, i.e. the fixed charge increase is combined with an offsetting reduction in the 
volumetric price component so that the average price undergoes no change. Using a dataset 
from a US electric utility, Lazar (2013) finds that consumption would increase by 7% in this 
case. Baatz (2017) reports that a 100% revenue-neutral increase in the fixed charge results in 
a consumption increase of 3–9% in the short run (and 10–20% in the long run) in the US. 
Finally, Pearce and Harris (2007), who also examine the US residential sector, provide 
further evidence for these results by showing how the revenue-neutral suppression of the 
fixed charge would result in a fall in consumption of 6.4% (as the marginal price increases). 
The findings of these ex-ante studies are aligned with standard economic theory and indicate 
that consumers respond to marginal prices, which implies that they identify fixed charges as 
a separate component of the cost. 
The only related ex-post evidence (i.e. impact evaluation) is provided by Ito and Zhang 
(2018), who analyze the effect of a recent reform involving the introduction of a TPT in 
China’s heating systems. Heating prices in China comprised just a fixed charge, dependent 
solely on dwelling size. The reform introduced a positive marginal price while 
simultaneously lowering the existing fixed charge. Through a quasi-experimental approach, 
the paper finds that treated households reduced their heating usage by 20% in response to the 
reform. They conclude that consumers distinguish between marginal and fixed costs, in line 
with the ex-ante evidence. However, for electricity demand in Spain, with a TPT scheme 
already in place, our results point in the opposite direction. This disparate result can be driven 
by critical differences in the two reforms under study: the Chinese reform introduced an 


 
6 
inexistent volumetric pricing, i.e., heating costs in China were totally unrelated to kWh 
consumed before the reform. By contrast, the Spanish reform consisted in relative changes 
within a mature TPT scheme. What is more, the Chinese reform involved increases in 
marginal and average prices, both of which led to decreased consumption. The Spanish 
reform also increased the average price, but this was accompanied by a reduction in the 
marginal price, thereby allowing for a thorough exploration of the role of the fixed charge in 
terms of cost recovery within a TPT scheme.  
To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first empirical evaluation of consumer 
responses to a non-revenue-neutral increase in the fixed charge of electricity in a TPT scheme 
of an advanced economy, a policy that is under growing consideration in many countries. 
Electric utilities have proposed shifting cost recoveries from the volumetric component price 
to the fixed-part component in at least 34 US states (Wood et al. 2016). The proposed fixed 
charge increase was more than 100% in 14 states (Whited et al. 2016). The proposed increase 
in the customer charge (fixed part fee) was 61% for a non-exhaustive list of 87 US electric 
utilities (Baatz 2017). In a similar vein, the European Commission (2015) has called for more 
efficient electricity tariffs by increasing the share of fixed costs covered by fixed-charge price 
components. In Europe, only Sweden, Netherlands and Spain have substantial fixed-price 
components, which finance over 75% of the industry’s fixed costs (Eurelectric 2016), in some 
cases expanded by recent reforms (2009 in Netherlands, 2013 in Spain and 2016 in Italy). 
Finally, Australia introduced new tariff structure rules in 2014; despite the flexibility shown 
by electric utilities in adapting these mechanisms to their network and customer 
characteristics, the trend has been to increase fixed charges while lowering volumetric 
charges (Australian Energy Regulator 2016).

1. Electricity pricing reform: the Spanish case 
Prior to the reform, Spanish residential electricity demand had been shrinking for a number 
of years due to a global tendency driven by more affordable energy-efficient home 
appliances, including the rapid emergence of LEDs (Davis 2017). Moreover, Spain had been 
hit hard by the 2008 global financial crisis, leading to a widespread reduction in consumption. 
The corresponding decline in electricity demand impacted the revenue of power companies 
to such an extent that it threatened the financial sustainability of the entire system. In 
response, an electricity pricing reform was introduced to achieve a “better balance between 
the pricing system and the industry’s cost structure” (Orden IET/1491/2013).

2. Data and research design 
Natural experiments have become the gold standard for making causal inferences in the social 
sciences, particularly in policy evaluation. However, one of the main challenges that has to  
be faced is that non-experimental data do not always provide a clean control group. Ideally, 
researchers would work on two randomly assigned samples, only one of which would be 
exposed to reform (the treated group). Only under such conditions can the causal effects of 
the reform be unequivocally identified. Here, however, all Spanish households are exposed 
to the same electricity pricing system and hence impacted by the reform shock. Additionally, 
microdata are usually gathered on a yearly basis, which means the policy impact is easily 
confounded with other, often unobservable, factors. This makes it virtually impossible to 
separate the policy effect from these confounders.

2.1 Data 
Data are taken, as indicated, from the Spanish HBS conducted by the Spanish statistics office, 
INE (2018). The HBS is the most comprehensive data source on Spanish household 
consumption choices, including income and other sociodemographic characteristics. Among 
other objectives, the survey is used to measure private consumption in National Accounting 
and to periodically update the expenditure weights of the Consumer Price Index.  
This high-quality survey reports annual data at the household level for the period 2006–2016 
on the physical quantity of consumption of certain goods and their corresponding 
expenditure, including electricity billing data. Given that we seek to determine the impact of 
the 2013 reform, we limit our sample to the 2011–2014 period to guarantee that no other 
significant events affect our data. For this reason, we use data from households that were 
interviewed between August 2011 and April 2014.7 Household participation in the survey is 
limited to two years, and only half of each year’s cohort is (randomly) invited to participate 
the second year. Our sample comprises a panel of 25,775 observations, and we exploit both 
the panel data structure and cross-sectional dimensions. 
HBS data on electricity consumption and expenditure are gathered through interviews 
conducted in a two-week period in which the interviewer collects information from the last 
paid bill, which refers to the previous month. These interviews are completed 26 times 
throughout the year for independently stratified samples (INE 2018) and thus produce 26 
homogenous subsamples per year that differ only in the date of the interview. As the survey 
is designed to provide annual aggregate information, both consumption and expenditure in 
the month before the interview are multiplied by the number of times the bill is paid in a year. 
As a result, although household electricity demand is observed on an annual basis, the

2.2. Identification strategy 
As the sample design of the Spanish HBS is stratified to provide identical groups of 
households across different rounds of interviewing, we exploit discontinuities in fixed-part 
price variation existing on different interview dates. This means these sampling groups are 
identical except for the interview date. We take advantage of the exogeneity of the survey 
interview date and of the survey sampling design to define a treated group and control group. 
After we control for potential confounders related with time, being interviewed before or 
after the reform approximates a random allocation of the reform in an experimental setting. 
The INE collects information by means of interviews conducted over two-week periods, 
during which all goods and services that are consumed are recorded, including any electricity 
bills. Sampling is stratified across 26 two-week periods in each year, which means we exploit 
26 homogenous subsamples per year to define our control and treated groups, that is, 
households whose electricity bills fall under the reform can be separated from those whose 
bills are unaffected by the reform. Because of the two-year panel structure of the HBS data, 
we exploit within-household variation. The key to our identification strategy is that a 
household’s second interview is held in the same two-week period of the year as that of the

interview conducted in the first year of participation; i.e., if household i was interviewed in 
the first two-week period of April in year t, it will be interviewed again in the same two-week 
period in t+1. Figure 3 presents our identification strategy in diagrammatic form.

by household i when T = 0, and qi1 denotes the amount consumed when T = 1, then we 
assume that 𝑞$ = 𝑞%$ + 𝑇(𝑞"$ −𝑞%$).  
The key here lies in the assumption of a sampling balance between the treated and control 
groups prior to the reform that, in our case, is guaranteed by the sample design. However, 
since there is no common support for control and treated groups after the reform (T=1), the 
only confounding differences that need to be controlled for are those that may arise from 
different weather conditions (or other time trends) that households faced in the month of the 
interview. Since weather is a function of geographical location and time, we use regional and 
quarterly dummies to control for its potential confounding effects. This guarantees that the 
effect of the policy is not confounded by these or other time trends.  
Households may have different retailers in different market regulation settings (regulated or 
‘free market’) or may have changed their situation in the second year. Although we cannot 
observe these details, a household’s effective electricity price (p=expenditure/consumption) 
is included in the model to capture all effects of specific household tariff choices. This, 
however, brings in potential endogeneity issues that will be addressed by means of an 
instrumental variable approach. The preceding steps would allow for the proper estimation 
of the causal effect of the reform.

The treatment coefficient is significant and negative in all specifications. According to the 
OLS specifications (column 1), treated households reduced their electricity consumption by 
about [withheld]%. When unobserved heterogeneity is controlled for (column 3), the ATE falls to 
15%. Instrumenting prices for potential endogeneity, the coefficients maintain their sign and 
magnitude although with larger standard errors (as expected in an IV approach). Therefore, 
when the fixed charge is raised and despite the reduction in the volumetric rate, households 
respond by reducing their electricity consumption. This first result is in line with studies 
finding that consumers react to average prices rather than to marginal prices (Ito, 2014). If 
consumers reacted to marginal prices, electricity consumption would increase as, in contrast 
to the average price, the volumetric rate fell (because of the reform itself and the evolution

of prices; see Figure A1). This means consumers use electricity at a level lower than that 
expected from a TPT scheme, thus with lower allocative efficiency. 
We conduct tests for under-identification, over-identification and weak instruments to test 
the validity of our instrument set. As shown in Table 2, neither the Kleibergen-Paap LM 
statistic nor the Hansen J statistic reject the model when heterogeneity is taken into account. 
The statistics for the Kleibergen-Paap LM test (chi-square with 3 degrees of freedom) vary 
from a maximum of 446.9 in models excluding fixed effects to a minimum of 21.37 in 
specifications including fixed effects. The null hypothesis of under-identification is therefore 
rejected at any level of significance.11 The results of the Hansen J and Sargan statistics of 
over-identifying restrictions (χ2 with 2 degrees of freedom) suggest that the null of validity 
of the instruments is only rejected for the specification in column 2. Yet, this rejection is 
overcome when household fixed effects are considered in column 4, thus reflecting correlated 
effects in the case of column 2. 
Although our set of instruments passes the under- and over-identification tests, GMM 
regressions might still suffer from the weak instruments problem (see Staiger and Stock 1997 
or Stock and Yogo 2005). If this were the case, the sampling distribution of GMM statistics 
would be non-normal, and standard GMM point estimates, hypothesis tests and confidence 
intervals would be unreliable. In this regard, according to Staiger and Stock’s (1997) rule of 
thumb, a first-stage F statistic below 10 in the fixed effects IV specification (column 4) 
suggests a problem of weak instruments. However, this is overcome here by the Cragg-
Donald statistics, the values of which are above Stock and Yogo’s (2005) critical value of 
10% in the case of the limited information maximum likelihood (LIML) estimation, 
indicating that the set of instruments are indeed not weak. Furthermore, since there is only a 
single endogenous regressor, we also conduct Montiel Olea and Pflueger’s (2013) robust 
weak instrument test (see also Andrews, Stock and Sun 2019). This shows that in the worst 
case bias of weak instruments, the bias for our preferred specification in column 4 is never 
higher than 20% of the OLS coefficient. This means the average [withheld]% reduction in 
electricity might range from a 9.2 to [withheld]% reduction if the worst case bias was present, while

However, while a reaction along these lines might be expected, the average contracted power

months of our sample fell 2% with respect to the non-treated sample. Even though we cannot

3.2 Distributional effects 
The impact of an increase in the fixed-price component of an energy good is, necessarily, 
heterogeneous across households and tends to be regressive. In the case of US residential 
natural gas, Borenstein and Davis (2012) show that a reform similar to that analyzed in this 
paper would be regressive, although financing fixed costs through markups of the volumetric 
rate was only mildly progressive (because of the correlation between gas consumption and 
income). In their study of heating-price reform in China, Ito and Zhang (2018) also find that 
moving from a plain tariff to a TPT was regressive. However, this is in part explained by the 
particular ex-ante situation where the plain tariff was a function of household dwelling size 
(and therefore progressive). In this section, we analyze the distributional incidence of the 
Spanish reform across households. 
Tables 4 and 5 show the results obtained when applying a quantile regression model (with 
bootstrapped standard errors) for consumption and expenditure, respectively. More 
specifically, they show the effects of the reform conditional on the deciles of electricity 
consumption. Since the coefficients in a quantile regression are not controlled by unobserved 
heterogeneity (as they are in the fixed effect model), the coefficients shown underestimate 
the actual impact, presumably in a similar way to the OLS estimations above.

consumption, i.e., some consumers cannot reduce electricity consumption further once their

3.3 Robustness checks 
To examine the robustness of the above estimates, we conduct two additional analyses. First, 
we test that the results are indeed driven by the reform and not by potential confounders, such 
as changes in home appliances or weather conditions. By controlling for these factors, we 
can see the extent to which our core estimates are driven by exogenous technology changes 
or the reform itself. Second, we perform a falsification test by estimating placebo reforms. 
These are also estimated by means of a non-parametric method (matching estimator) so that 
we test our results using a different methodology. 
As shown in the previous section, our results are robust to different specifications and time 
spans. However, in recent years, not only have home appliances become more energy-
efficient, but new labeling regulations have made this information more readily available to 
consumers who are better placed to optimize energy-saving investments. To ensure that our 
estimates are not being confounded by this technology trend, Table 7 includes additional 
controls related to household investment in new home appliances, particularly investments 
in (i) all kinds of home appliances, (ii) small electric appliances such as bulbs, and (iii) 
changes in the heating or water boiler. Compared to our core estimations, the reform 


 
27 
coefficient remains virtually unchanged. The panel data model in column 1 shows that the 
reform coefficient remains at -13.7% when the sample is limited to households without a 
positive investment in any set of home appliances.15 For cross-sectional models, the results 
remain at -10%, as for the previous specifications. Investment in home appliances does, 
however, show a significant impact on electricity consumption (column 2), albeit with a 
positive impact on electricity demand. 
If our estimates were confounded by weather conditions, and hence driven by more or less 
intense use of heating, by restricting our sample to households whose heating and water boiler 
energy sources are non-electric (column 3), we would see a change in the reform coefficient 
that would indicate that the shift in consumption was not driven by the reform but by a change 
in weather conditions. This is not the case, as the reform coefficient remains at -13%. In the 
same vein, as our period of analysis ends in April 2014, the treated group does not include 
the April to August period, unlike the control group (see Figure 1). Therefore, the estimated 
effect of the reform could be the product of selected months (those “months behavior” are

model restricting the sample so that control and treatment groups have the same months, that 
is, we remove control-group households that were interviewed from April to August. Again, 
the coefficient remains virtually identical (-14%).

4. Discussion and conclusion  
Full cost recovery is critical to ensure reliable and sustainable electricity supplies. Moreover, 
it is a principle that underpins the electricity tariff setting. As the power sector has been 
subject to major transformations in recent years, increasing the share of fixed costs recovered 
through the fixed charge to consumers via their electricity bill (as opposed to markups at the 
volumetric rate) has become commonplace on regulatory agendas in many countries. Using 
the data collection method employed by the HBS to analyze the 2013 major Spanish 
electricity reform, this paper provides quasi-experimental evidence on the reduction of 
electricity consumption when households face an increase in the fixed charge of their 
electricity bill, despite a simultaneous decrease in the marginal price. This strongly suggests 
that consumers fail to discriminate between marginal and fixed costs and thus optimize 


 
31 
consumption at the average price rather than the marginal price. This result has timely and 
far-reaching policy implications for upcoming reforms to electricity pricing. 
First, suboptimal behavior of this type results in lower electricity expenditure, which in turn 
significantly undermines fixed-cost recovery. To the extent that consumers react to a (higher) 
average price, as opposed to a (lower) marginal price, the reduction in consumption cuts 
household expenditure to the point that the revenues raised from the higher fixed charge may 
fail to offset the revenue loss from the contraction in electricity demand. The Spanish 
electricity sector ended up in a worse financial situation than if the 2013 reform had not been 
introduced. 
An alternative hypothesis that we unfortunately cannot test directly is that households 
responded to the reform by adapting their contracted power, and therefore reduced their 
electricity expenditure. Even if this was the case, which is not supported by the information 
provided by the Spanish energy regulator (CNMC), this would not explain the sizeable 
reduction observed in electricity consumption unless the phenomenon was linked to radical 
changes in habits or to reduced energy needs related to energy-efficiency investments that 
were discarded by our robustness checks. 
Second, the impact of a reform of this kind is heterogeneous across households and electricity 
price increases brought about by a rise in the fixed-change component are regressive, in line 
with the general findings of the literature. Moreover, a quantile regression shows that the 
highest electricity consumers reduce their electricity consumption and expenditure the most, 
while the lowest electricity consumers fail to respond at all. 
Third, allocative efficiency, the other ultimate aim of a TPT scheme, is not achieved as 
consumer misperceptions of the fixed price component result in their reaction to a higher 
average price rather than to the true marginal price. As a result, electricity consumption is 
lower than it would have been had consumers reacted to the marginal price. From a climate 
policy perspective, this lower level of consumption can be considered a positive side effect, 
as it reduces energy-related emissions. However, from a welfare perspective, this is positive 
only if the pre-existing electricity price was below its social marginal cost and thus the reform 
implicitly solves this issue (thanks to consumer misperceptions). If the resulting price signal 


 
32 
is higher than the social marginal cost, this will entail lower-than-optimal electricity use that 
might actually work against the increased electrification that is likely to be necessary for 
successful decarbonization.  
Overall, we find robust evidence that the reform led to a reduction in residential electricity 
demand. Given our quasi-experimental research design, applied here in a structural model, 
and the representativeness of our sample, the policy implications are clearly not limited to 
Spain and should be of interest to those about to undertake similar reforms. However, a 
number of key research questions remain unanswered by our analysis. Specifically, why do 
consumers fail to identify marginal costs? A potential hypothesis, and one confirmed by the 
literature, is that of imperfect information: first in relation to prices, as non-linear tariffs are 
complex and require considerable cognitive effort to be understood; and second, in relation 
to quantities, as most consumers are unable to track their cumulative electricity consumption 
during the billing period.  
A further potential driver that may help explain our results is the salience of the reform itself. 
It is well-established in the literature that, in the case of gasoline, the salience of new taxes 
can impact demand, regardless of actual prices (Rivers and Schaufele 2015; Li et al. 2014; 
Davis and Killian 2011). Likewise, tax persistence compared to market prices also affects 
demand (Tiezzi and Verde 2019; Davis and Killian 2011). Both salience and persistence 
result in a significantly greater impact on demand than is otherwise expected from market 
price elasticities. However, little research has been conducted to date on these issues in the 
electricity market, with the sole exception of Gilbert and Zivin (2014). Indeed, it could well 
be that both salience and persistence reinforced the demand response identified here. In all 
circumstances, correcting imperfect information at the household level would undoubtedly 
improve market efficiency in both the short- and long-run.
