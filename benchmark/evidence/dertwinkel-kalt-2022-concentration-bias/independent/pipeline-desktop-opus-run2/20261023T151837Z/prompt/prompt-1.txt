Abstract (first 500 chars): Many intertemporal trade-offs are unbalanced: while the advantages of options are concen-
trated in a few periods, the disadvantages are dispersed over numerous periods. We provide
novel experimental evidence for “concentration bias”, the tendency to overweight advantages
that are concentrated in time. Subjects commit to too much overtime work that is dispersed
over multiple days in exchange for a bonus that is concentrated in time: concentration bias
increases subjects’ willingness to work by 2
Methodology Summary: - **Design and identification (consumption experiment, Main-Treatment):** This is a preregistered lab experiment (AEA RCT registry 4446 and 4341) with a within-subjects design. Each subject first completes 8 balanced trade-offs in random order. In trade-off j, extra real-effort tasks on workday j only are traded against a voucher raise r_j. This elicits indifference points x̃ = (x̃1,…,x̃8). The subject then faces one unbalanced trade-off: extra tasks x̃_t + d on all 8 workdays against the total raise R = Σr_j (€31.30) on the restaurant day (t = 9). The indifference point d̃ is the nonparametric, subject-level absolute measure of concentration bias. Discounted utility predicts d̃ = 0, while the focusing model (Kőszegi & Szeidl 2013) predicts d̃ > 0.
- **Parametrisation:** Mandatory tasks e = (129,132,143,115,121,117,135,127). The base voucher is v1 = €7.50. The raises are r = (2.90,2.70,3.10,2.70,3.20,2.90,3.10,3.20) €. Subjects pick 8 workdays from 20 business days and the restaurant day from 7 dates. The real-effort task transcribes 6 numbers into letters, with 10 practice tasks. Three subjects per session (27–32 participants) are randomly paid by having their chosen plan implemented, plus €100. Every subject gets €10 plus arithmetic-task earnings.
- **Elicitation:** Each trade-off has 126 binary choices: x ∈ {0,…,125} in balanced trade-offs and d ∈ {−63,…,62} in the unbalanced one. Subjects make up to 9 of them directly with an adaptive bisection procedure; the first choice is random, each later one is the midpoint of the remaining interval, and the other choices are implied. This enforces single switching. The indifference point is defined as the largest number of extra tasks accepted. Every choice is equally likely to be payoff-relevant.
- **Sample restrictions and corner handling:** As preregistered, subjects with any corner choice in the balanced trade-offs are excluded, leaving N = 100 in Main-Treatment (200 per pair of conditions in Table 3). For upper-corner choices in the unbalanced trade-off (27% of subjects), d̃ is proxied three ways: the lower bound (d̃ = 62), the upper bound (a manually stated, unincentivised value) and the midpoint, which is the preferred proxy.
- **Outcome measures:** The absolute measure is d̃. The relative measure is d̃_rel = (1/8) Σ_t d̃/(e_t + x̃_t), which is the headline measure: midpoint-based, expressed as a proportion, not standardised.
- **Estimation and inference:**
  - Table 2 reports OLS means of d̃ (31.64 / 37.61 / 43.58 for the lower bound, midpoint and upper bound) and of d̃_rel (0.190 / 0.224 / 0.259), each from a constant-only regression with robust (heteroskedasticity-consistent) SEs, N = 100.
  - Column 4 of Table 2 is a Tobit regression on the lower-bound d̃ censored at 62, giving 37.094 (SE 3.658).
  - The share of subjects with d̃ > 0 (88%) is tested against 50%.
  - The other preregistered tests are as follows:
    - Demographics are used as controls in heterogeneity regressions.
    - Cognitive proxies (Raven, CRT, arithmetic) are used for heterogeneity.
    - Response times are also examined.
- **Between-subject conditions (Table 3, OLS, robust SEs, N = 200 each):**
  - Mechanism-Treatment frames the voucher as a sum of parts. It gives d̃_rel = 0.075, which is 0.149 lower than Main-Treatment.
  - Donation-Treatment uses a charity donation as the benefit. It gives 0.135, a difference of 0.158 versus its control.
  - The Control conditions (Main, Mechanism and Donation) repeat balanced trade-off j = 1, 4 or 8 in place of the unbalanced one, to test preference stability. They give −0.023 and −0.014, and the treatment-minus-control differences are 0.247 and 0.090.
  - Within each pair, conditions are assigned at random within sessions, stratified by ranking subjects on their mean balanced-choice tasks and randomising within neighbouring pairs.
- **Money experiment:** Money-Main (n = 185) and Money-Mechanism (n = 189) use convex time budget allocations (Andreoni & Sprenger 2012) with a positive interest rate. The outcome is the savings rate, i.e. the share allocated to the later payment. It is compared between balanced choices (both payments concentrated) and unbalanced choices (the earlier or later payoff dispersed over 2, 4 or 8 dates). All subjects face identical choices. Results are reported as percentage-point differences, for example −5.7 p.p. when the later payoff is dispersed and +6.8 p.p. when the earlier one is, and 8.1 / 6.6 / 3.7 p.p. for 8 / 4 / 2 dates. No train/test split or cross-validation is used (this is experimental inference, not ML).
