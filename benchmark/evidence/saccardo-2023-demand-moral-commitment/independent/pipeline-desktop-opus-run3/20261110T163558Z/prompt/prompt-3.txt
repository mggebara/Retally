## Paper
Title: American Economic Review 2023, 113(2): 396–429
Methodology: - **Design / estimation approach:** Preregistered online experiments (aspredicted.org) with N = 9,323 advisors (Table 1) in an advice game. An advisor recommends product A (pays $2 with prob. 0.6) or product B (pays $2 with prob. 0.8 if high quality, 0.4 if low quality; prior 0.5) to an uninformed client. The advisor gets a $0.15 commission for one product and sees one ball drawn from B as a quality signal. The key outcome is a binary indicator for recommending the incentivized product, analysed separately by conflict of interest (commission on B with a $0 signal, or commission on A with a $2 signal) vs. no conflict. Treatment effects are estimated with regressions of this indicator on information-order indicators: assigned to "See Incentive First" vs. "Assess Quality First", interacted with the advisor's preferred order. The design-variation controls in all regressions are probabilistic vs. standard payment and left/right screen position of the incentivized product (fn. 14). A stylised dual-self attention model (Bénabou–Tirole 2002) generates Hypotheses 1–2.
- **Experiments / treatments (Table 1):**
  - NoChoice: See Incentive First (N = 152) vs. Assess Quality First (N = 147), exogenously assigned.
  - Choice: advisors choose their information order, which is implemented with 75% probability (50% their choice + 50% × a 50-50 randomisation). This gives random assignment conditional on preference. Arms: Choice Free—Professionals (N = 712; self-reported finance/insurance and legal workers from Prolific + CloudResearch, pooled because p = 0.308 for choice and p = 0.820 for recommendations); Choice Free (AMT/CloudResearch, N = 2,574); Incentive First Costly (N = 1,562); Quality First Costly (N = 1,067). The cost is forgoing $0.05 (one-third of the commission) to get the costly order.
  - Robustness arms (online appendix): High Stakes 10-fold (275) and 100-fold (110), Replication (385), Deterministic implementation (369).
  - Choice Stakes: advisor incentive of $0.01 / $0.15 / $0.30 (N = 483 / 511 / 478), with seeing the incentive first always costly.
  - Information Architect third-party experiment: IA-Advisor (245) vs. IA-Client (253), with a $0.15 IA payment aligned with the advisor or the client.
- **Assignment / payoffs:** The commission is predetermined to maximise conflicts: a low-quality B is always paired with a commission on B, and a high-quality B with a commission on A. This gives about 70% of advisors a conflict of interest. Most arms pay $0.15 and match 1 in 10 advisors with a client. Professionals and a Choice Free subsample get a probabilistic scheme: 1 in 100 advisors is paid a $15 commission with product payoffs scaled to $0/$20. The base pay is $0.50 or $1 for a 5–7 minute study. Clients (N = 924) were recruited later; 84% followed the advice.
- **Additional measures:**
  - Posterior belief that B is low quality: an incentivised choice of one of ten 10-pp intervals ($0.15 for a correct range; $15 in the 1/100 arms), plus an exact 0–100 number.
  - Moral costs / "selfishness": a 5-decision multiple price list recommending Y (always the incentivized product, with a $0 signal) vs. X paying $2 with prob. 1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4 and 0. This is used as a **standardised** selfishness measure and was not collected for the Professionals sample.
  - Blinding take-up (a stronger commitment) in wave 3 of the Choice experiment.
- **Sample restrictions:** Following the preregistrations, the main analyses keep attentive advisors who gave consistent multiple-price-list responses, so multiple switchers are excluded. Online Appendix C shows the results are qualitatively similar with them included. Further exclusion criteria are in online Appendix B. The Choice experiment was run in three separately preregistered waves, which are pooled.
