## Paper Information
Title: The Economic Journal, 132 (August), 2290–2307 https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueab105 C⃝The Author(s) 2021. Published by Oxford University Press. All rights
Abstract (first 500 chars): Advance Access Publication Date: 31 December 2021 PATIENCE, RISK-TAKING, AND HUMAN CAPITAL INVESTMENT ACROSS COUNTRIES∗ Eric A. Hanushek, Lavinia Kinne, Philipp Lergetporer and Ludger Woessmann Patience and risk-taking—two preference components that steer intertemporal decision-making—are fun- damental to human capital investment decisions. To understand how they contribute to international skill
Methodology Summary: - **Data and sample**: Student-level PISA micro data (math, science, reading; 15-year-olds) from seven waves, 2000–2018, merged with country-level Global Preference Survey (GPS, Falk et al. 2018; ~1,000 respondents per country, surveyed 2012). Baseline cross-country sample: the 49 countries that appear in both PISA and the GPS (1,992,276 students, 263 country-by-wave cells). Migrant sample: waves 2003–2018 only (the 2000 wave drops out because country of origin is missing), keeping students whose origin country PISA identifies and that has GPS data. That gives 80,398 students from 58 origin countries in 48 residence countries (a wider migrant definition gives up to 145,506).
- **Preprocessing**: Each GPS preference domain (patience, risk-taking, plus positive reciprocity, negative reciprocity, altruism, trust) is z-standardised within the relevant analytical sample and then collapsed to country means. PISA scores are divided by 100, so the outcome is in international student SD units. The preference regressors are therefore in country-level SD units of the standardised GPS measure, and the coefficients themselves are unstandardised. Imputation dummies flag missing controls. Migrant status: both parents born abroad. First-generation migrants are assigned their own country of birth; second-generation migrants are assigned their father's country of origin. A test-taking-effort proxy is built for every wave: item non-response, the share of unanswered questions in the student background questionnaire that follows the test.
- **Baseline cross-country specification (Eq. 1, Table 1)**: Weighted least squares at the student level. T_ict = β1·Patience_c + β2·Risk_c + α1·B_ict + μ_t + ε_ict. The dependent variable is the PISA math score/100. The key regressors are the country's GPS patience and risk-taking, entered separately (cols 1–2) and jointly (col 3, preferred). Controls B: student gender, age, migration status, and imputation dummies. Wave fixed effects μ_t are included. No country fixed effects (the treatment varies only at the country level), and the specification is deliberately parsimonious: proximate family, school and institutional inputs are treated as bad controls.
- **Weights and inference (baseline)**: Regressions are weighted by students' within-country sampling probabilities, rescaled so that each country carries equal weight. Standard errors are robust and clustered at the country level (49 clusters).
- **Baseline extensions and robustness**: Col 4 adds the other four GPS preference measures. Cols 5–6 estimate natives and migrants separately and test the difference between the two subsamples. Cols 7–8 add individual item non-response and then also its country mean. In the appendix: OECD vs non-OECD samples, science and reading outcomes, only the first PISA wave after the GPS, WVS and Hofstede preference measures instead of GPS, and girls vs boys. A country-level version regresses wave-pooled average math scores on the preferences, with bivariate and added-variable plots (Figure 1).
- **Migrant identification specification (Eq. 2, Table 2)**: OLS on migrant students only. T_ioct = δ1·Patience_o + δ2·Risk_o + γ1·B_ioct + θ_c×μ_t + ε_ioct. The preferences are those of the origin country. The specification includes a full set of residence-country × wave fixed effects (180 cells), so identification comes only from within-residence-country variation across origin countries. Controls: gender, age, a dummy for an OECD origin country, and imputation dummies. Standard errors are clustered by country of origin (58 clusters). Variants: adding the other four origin-country GPS preferences (col 4); controlling for individual and origin-country-mean item non-response (cols 5–7); splitting the sample by whether the student speaks the residence-country language at home (cols 8–9, with a difference test).
- **Selection robustness (Table 3)**, each added to Eq. 2: continent-of-origin fixed effects; within-origin-country SDs of patience and risk-taking from individual GPS data weighted by sampling probability; geographic distance between the most populous cities (Mayer–Zignago, in 1,000 km); interactions of each preference with the absolute origin–residence preference difference, all variables demeaned; and a migrant-selectivity measure, the percentile of the origin-country schooling distribution that the average migrant in each residence country comes from. The last two have restricted samples (29,019 and 39,725 students). Appendix checks: first vs second generation, age at migration, alternative migrant definitions, and controls for home-country economic conditions and family socio-economic background.
- **Channels (Table 4)**: Country-level OLS (N = 49) of four aggregated proximate-input indices (family, school, institutional, and a residual/productivity term) on patience and risk-taking, with robust (unclustered) standard errors. There are two versions. The upper bound builds the input aggregation weights without the preference controls. The lower bound builds them from an aggregation regression that also controls for the two preferences. The paper treats this part as descriptive only, not as a causal mediation analysis. There is no train/test split or cross-validation; inference throughout rests on coefficient significance from clustered or robust SEs, plus R² for explained cross-country variance.
