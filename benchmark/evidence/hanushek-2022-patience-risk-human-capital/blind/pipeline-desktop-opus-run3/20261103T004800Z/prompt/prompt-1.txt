## Paper Information
Title: The Economic Journal, 132 (August), 2290–2307 https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueab105 C⃝The Author(s) 2021. Published by Oxford University Press. All rights
Abstract (first 500 chars): Advance Access Publication Date: 31 December 2021 PATIENCE, RISK-TAKING, AND HUMAN CAPITAL INVESTMENT ACROSS COUNTRIES∗ Eric A. Hanushek, Lavinia Kinne, Philipp Lergetporer and Ludger Woessmann Patience and risk-taking—two preference components that steer intertemporal decision-making—are fun- damental to human capital investment decisions. To understand how they contribute to international skill
Methodology Summary: - **Data and samples:** Student-level PISA data (math, science and reading for 15-year-olds) from 7 waves, 2000–2018 (2000, 2003, 2006, 2009, 2012, 2015, 2018), merged with country-level Global Preference Survey (GPS; Falk et al., 2018; about 1,000 respondents per country, surveyed in 2012). The baseline cross-country sample is the 49 countries covered by both PISA and the GPS: 1,992,276 students in 263 country-by-wave cells. The migrant sample covers waves 2003–2018 (the 2000 wave is dropped because it lacks country of origin). It contains 80,398 migrant students from 58 origin countries with GPS data, living in 48 residence countries (up to 145,506 under a wider migrant definition).
- **Preprocessing:** Each GPS preference measure (patience, risk-taking, and also positive reciprocity, negative reciprocity, altruism and trust) is z-standardised at the individual level within the analysis sample, then collapsed to country means. PISA scores are divided by 100, so the outcome is in international SD units; the dependent variable is in levels and is not standardised. Imputation dummies are used for missing controls. Test-taking effort is proxied by item non-response: the share of unanswered questions in the student background questionnaire that follows the test. It is built for every wave, at the individual level and as a country (or country-of-origin) mean. Migrants are students whose parents were both born abroad. First-generation migrants are assigned their own country of birth; second-generation migrants get their father's country of origin.
- **Baseline cross-country specification (Eq. 1, Table 1):** Pooled student-level OLS: T_ict = β1·Patience_c + β2·Risk_c + α1·B_ict + μ_t + ε_ict. T is the PISA math score divided by 100. Patience and risk-taking are country-level GPS z-scores, in SD units, and are entered jointly (preferred spec, col. 3). Controls B are student gender, age and migration status, plus imputation dummies and wave fixed effects μ_t; there are no country fixed effects. Variants:
  - patience alone (col. 1) and risk-taking alone (col. 2);
  - adding the four other GPS preferences (col. 4);
  - natives only (col. 5) and migrants only (col. 6), with the difference between them tested;
  - adding individual item non-response (col. 7), then also the country-mean item non-response (col. 8).
- **Weighting and inference (baseline):** Regressions are weighted by students' sampling probabilities within countries, rescaled so each country gets equal weight. Standard errors are robust and clustered at the country level throughout (49 clusters). Fit is reported as R². Figure 1 adds country-level bivariate and added-variable plots of mean math score (pooled across waves) on patience and risk-taking, with the country-level R² shown.
- **Migrant (epidemiological) specification (Eq. 2, Tables 2–3):** OLS on migrant students only: T_ioct = δ1·Patience_o + δ2·Risk_o + γ1·B_ioct + θ_c×μ_t + ε_ioct. Preferences are those of the origin country o. The model has 180 residence-country-by-wave fixed effects, so identification comes only from variation within residence country and wave. Controls are gender, age, a dummy for OECD country of origin, and imputation dummies. Standard errors are clustered at the country-of-origin level. Table 2 variants:
  - patience alone (col. 1), risk-taking alone (col. 2), both together (col. 3, preferred), and adding the other four origin-country GPS preferences (col. 4);
  - controls for item non-response (cols. 5–7: individual, origin-country mean, and both);
  - split by whether the student speaks the residence-country language at home (cols. 8 and 9), with the difference tested.
- **Migrant selectivity checks (Table 3):** All columns keep the residence-country-by-wave fixed effects and the controls. They add, in turn:
  - continent-of-origin fixed effects (col. 1);
  - within-origin-country SDs of patience and risk-taking from the individual GPS data, weighted by sampling probability (col. 2);
  - geographic distance between the most populous cities of origin and residence country, in 1,000 km (CEPII; col. 3);
  - preferences interacted with the absolute patience and risk-taking gaps between origin and residence country, all variables demeaned (col. 5; col. 4 is the same reduced sample without the interactions);
  - migrant educational selectivity, measured as the percentile of the origin-country schooling distribution that the average migrant in each residence country comes from (col. 7; col. 6 is the matching reduced sample).
  The online appendix also covers OECD/non-OECD splits, science and reading, girls and boys, migrant generation and age at migration, alternative migrant definitions, and WVS/Hofstede measures in place of the GPS.
- **Channels analysis (Table 4):** Country-level OLS on 49 countries, with robust standard errors. Each of four aggregated proximate-input indices (family, school, institutional, and a residual or productivity term) is regressed on patience and risk-taking. Upper-bound estimates build the input aggregation without the preferences; lower-bound estimates build it while controlling for them. This analysis is described as descriptive, not causal mediation.
- **No ML-style validation:** There is no train/test split, no cross-validation and no hyperparameters. Evaluation is based on coefficient significance with clustered standard errors, R², and tests of differences between subsamples.
