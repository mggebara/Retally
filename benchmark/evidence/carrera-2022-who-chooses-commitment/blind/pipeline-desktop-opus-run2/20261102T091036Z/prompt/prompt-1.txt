Methodology Summary: - **Design / sample:** Online elicitation followed by 4 weeks of observed gym attendance (from membership swipe records) at a university-affiliated fitness facility in a Midwest US city. Eligibility: age 18+ and a membership held over the past 8 weeks. Three recruitment waves (Oct 2015 – Mar 2016) with 350, 528 and 414 participants; the analysis sample is N = 1,248. The 4-week incentive periods start on the Monday after the online component and are timed to avoid academic breaks.
- **Information treatment (randomized 50/50 before any elicitation):** Wave 1 showed a graph of the participant's weekly visits over the past 20 weeks. Waves 2–3 used an enhanced version: participants also typed in their own estimate of average weekly visits while viewing the graph, and were told that previous-wave participants had overestimated their attendance by about 4 visits over 4 weeks (about 1 per week). Controls went straight to the elicitations.
- **Elicitations:** Unincentivized "best guess" of visits over the next 4 weeks, a goal number of visits and the perceived probability of meeting it. Then 6 piece-rate incentives ($1, $2, $3, $5, $7, $12 per visit), shown in random order on separate pages. For each, participants gave forecast visits (0–28) and WTP via slider, which ran from $0 to 30× the rate, with a fill-in box if they hit the slider maximum. WTP was elicited by BDM, with two comprehension-check questions on how the slider works. The comparison fixed payments were drawn from a mixture: U[$0,$7] with weight 0.99 and U[full slider range] with weight 0.01.
- **Commitment contracts:** Binary choices between an unconditional $80 and $80 paid only if attendance meets a threshold. Waves 1–2: "8+, 12+, 16+" (more) and "7−, 11−, 15−" (fewer), for 6 choices. Wave 3: only 12+ and 11−, plus the elicited probability of meeting each threshold.
- **Random assignment of incentives:** One question per participant was chosen at random, with probabilities set by wave. Wave 1: $0 (vs. a $20 fixed payment), $2 and $7 piece rates at 0.33 each. Wave 2: $0 33%, $2 33%, $5 16.5%, $7 16.5%. Wave 3: $0, $7 piece rate, and $80 conditional on 12+ visits at 33% each; the $80 conditional group got it only if they preferred it to $0. The remaining 1% was spread over all questions. Treatment effects on attendance compare the assigned incentive groups against the $0 group.
- **Sample restriction:** 44 observations where incentive assignment was endogenous to WTP are dropped from all analyses: 12 where the fixed-payment draw exceeded $7, and 32 where WTP fell inside the $0–$7 range.
- **Reduced-form measure (behavior change premium):** For adjacent incentive levels, BCP(p,Δ) = [w(p+Δ) − w(p)]/Δ − [α̃(p+Δ) + α̃(p)]/2, built from stated WTP and forecast attendance. It is expressed per $1 of piece-rate incentive and is unbiased at the population level under mean-zero valuation noise. Take-up of the "more" and "fewer" contracts is related to the BCP, to proxies for sophistication and to the information treatment. Correlation between "more" and "less" take-up is used to test for stochastic valuation errors (an imperfect-perception / random-utility model, logistic two-stage Luce).
- **Structural estimation:** Quasi-hyperbolic (β–δ) model with partial naivete (perceived β̃, after O'Donoghue–Rabin 2001), with δ = 1, utility quasilinear in money, stochastic per-period attendance costs and delayed benefit b per visit. It is estimated from forecast attendance, actual attendance and WTP moments under randomized piece rates, separately from contract take-up, and validated with out-of-sample tests. Welfare is assessed under a long-run criterion: consumer surplus from commitment contracts compared with linear per-visit subsidies scaled to produce the same average increase in attendance. No ML-style train/test split or k-fold cross-validation is used.
