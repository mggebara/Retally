Abstract (first 500 chars): This paper investigates whether offers of commitment contracts, in the form of self-imposed

Methodology Summary: - **Design and sample:** A field experiment with members of a fitness facility in a large Midwest US city that is affiliated with a private university (N = 1,248 in the analysis). Three recruitment waves ran from October 2015 to March 2016, with 350, 528 and 414 participants enrolled. Eligibility: age over 18 and a gym membership held over the past 8 weeks. Each participant completed an online elicitation, followed by 4 weeks of gym attendance observed from swipe data, starting the Monday after the online component. All payments were made after the 4-week period.
- **Randomized information treatment (50% probability, before any elicitation):** In wave 1, participants saw a graph of their weekly visits over the past 20 weeks. In waves 2–3 they also had to estimate their own average weekly visits while viewing the graph, and were told that prior participants had overestimated attendance by 4 visits over 4 weeks (1 per week). Controls went straight to the elicitations.
- **Elicitations:**
  - An unincentivized "best guess" of visits over the next 4 weeks, plus a goal and the probability of meeting it.
  - For each of six piece-rates ($1, $2, $3, $5, $7, $12 per day; pages in random order), a forecast of visits (0–28) and WTP for the incentive. WTP was given on a slider from $0 to 30× the rate, with a fill-in box when the slider was at its maximum. It was elicited with an incentive-compatible BDM mechanism, with two comprehension checks.
  - Binary commitment-contract choices of $80 unconditional vs. $80 conditional on the visit count. Waves 1–2 offered thresholds of ≥8, ≥12 and ≥16 visits and ≤7, ≤11 and ≤15 visits. Wave 3 offered only ≥12 and ≤11, plus elicited probabilities of meeting each threshold.
- **Random assignment of incentives (one question chosen per person):**
  - Wave 1: $0 (vs. a $20 fixed payment), $2/day and $7/day at about 33% each.
  - Wave 2: $0 at 33%, $2 at 33%, $5 at 16.5%, $7 at 16.5%.
  - Wave 3: $0, $7/day, and $80 conditional on 12+ visits (for those who preferred it to $0) at 33% each.
  - In all waves, the remaining 1% was drawn from all other questions.
  - BDM fixed payments were drawn from a mixture: 0.99 Uniform[$0, $7] and 0.01 Uniform over the full slider range.
- **Sample restriction:** 44 observations with endogenous incentive assignment are excluded from all analyses. These are 12 cases where the fixed-payment draw exceeded $7 and 32 cases where the WTP fell inside the $0–$7 draw range.
- **Reduced-form measures and estimation:**
  - The behavior change premium per $1 of incentives: BCP(p, Δ) = [w(p+Δ) − w(p)]/Δ − [α̃(p+Δ) + α̃(p)]/2, computed from adjacent piece-rate WTPs and forecasts. Population means are tested against 0; BCP > 0 implies perceived β̃ < 1.
  - Treatment effects of incentives on visits are estimated by comparing randomized incentive groups against the $0 group.
  - Effects of the information treatment on forecasts, BCP and take-up are estimated by comparing treatment and control.
  - Commitment take-up is related to BCP and to sophistication proxies.
  - Take-up correlation: the correlation between "more" and "less" contract take-up at each threshold.
- **Structural model:** Quasi-hyperbolic (β–δ) discounting with partial naivete (β̃) in the O'Donoghue–Rabin style. δ = 1, utility is quasilinear in money, and attendance costs are stochastic with a delayed benefit b per visit. Actual β, perceived β̃ (separately by information arm) and b are estimated from the piece-rate moments: forecasted and actual attendance curves and WTP. The model is validated with out-of-sample tests, including predicting behavior under the $80/12-visit contract.
- **Welfare:** Consumer surplus from commitment contracts is computed for those who take them up, under the long-run criterion and allowing for stochastic valuation errors in take-up. Social efficiency of the contracts is compared with linear per-visit subsidies scaled to produce the same average increase in attendance.
