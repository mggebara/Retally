## Paper Information
Title: UvA-DARE is a service provided by the library of the University of Amsterdam (https://dare.uva.nl)
Abstract (first 500 chars): Yellow vests, pessimistic beliefs, and carbon tax aversion Douenne, T.; Fabre, A. DOI 10.1257/pol.20200092 Publication date 2022
Methodology Summary: - **Data / sample:** Online survey of a representative sample of 3,002 French respondents (Bilendi panel, Feb–Mar 2019), with quotas on gender, age (5 brackets), education (4), socioprofessional category (8), town size (5) and region (9), relaxed by 5–10%. Inattentive and fastest respondents were excluded. Observations are weighted to correct small sample–population differences (e.g., in education). The survey contains independent randomized information treatments: climate change / particulate matter info (2×2); "scientists agree a carbon tax would be effective" (half the sample); customized win/lose feedback (1/2 feedback only, 1/3 progressivity info only, 1/6 both, so 2/3 get feedback, n = 1,968); and random assignment to targeted-dividend variants (eligibility for bottom 20/30/40/50% of income, thresholds €780/1,140/1,430/1,670 per month, payments €550/360/270/220 per year; Table 1).
- **Policy and objective net gains:** The main reform is a +€50/tCO2 carbon tax with a uniform €110/year dividend per adult (at most 2 adults per household). Sectoral variants: housing (+13% gas, +15% heating oil, €50 dividend) and transport (+€0.11/L gasoline, +€0.13/L diesel, €60 dividend). Objective net gain is eq. (1): γ_h = N_h^a·D − ΔE_h^transport − ΔE_h^housing. It uses price elasticities of −0.4 (transport) and −0.2 (housing), assumes consumers bear 80% of incidence, and expresses everything per consumption unit (Eurostat c.u.). The distributions come from Insee BdF 2011 matched with ENTD 2008 (>10,000 households), with expenditures inflated using National Accounts.
- **Respondent-specific prediction (a train/test-style validation):** ΔE^transport is computed directly from the distance and fuel economy each respondent reports. ΔE^housing is predicted from an OLS regression of housing energy expenditure on household characteristics estimated on the housing survey (EL 2013). Accuracy is checked out of sample on a different survey (BdF consumer survey): the win/lose category is correctly predicted for 83.4% of predicted winners and 83.4% of predicted losers (made symmetric), which is the "five cases out of six" shown to respondents. Accuracy is 99% when |gain| > €110/c.u.
- **Bias measurement:** Subjective gains are elicited in intervals scaled by c.u. and converted to numbers using within-bin averages from the Insee data. Subjective and objective CDFs are compared (Fig. 2). Reported figures include average objective gains (€18, €6 and €24 per c.u.) vs subjective (−€61, −€43, −€89); 70% objective winners vs 14% subjective; median gap €116; and 89% of respondents underestimating their gain (77% under inelastic expenditures).
- **Determinants of large bias (Table 2):** Dependent variable is "large bias", a dummy for |γ̂ − g| > €110/year per c.u. Specifications are linear probability OLS (col 1), logit with average marginal effects (col 2), and OLS adding initial approval of the tax (col 3). Key regressors are Yellow Vests attitude (omitted: opposes; PNR/understands/supports/is part), environmentalist, and left–right leaning (omitted: extreme left). Controls are sociodemographics and political leaning (listed in the online Appendix). N = 3,002; R² = 0.061 (col 1) and 0.098 (col 3). Plain standard errors are reported in parentheses, with no clustering mentioned. Multiple testing uses Benjamini–Hochberg FDR at 5%, via F-tests of joint nullity per categorical variable and for household-composition and income triplets.
- **Belief updating (Table 3):** For the 1,968 respondents who received feedback, the share whose post-feedback win/lose belief matches the feedback is tabulated by initial belief, with 95% binomial confidence intervals. For example, 25.1% align with "win" feedback vs 85.7% with "lose" feedback, and 12.2% of initial "losers" given win feedback switch to win. The causal effect of win vs lose feedback on beliefs is estimated with a sharp RDD at the zero threshold of the continuous predicted net gain γ̂.
- **Causal effect of beliefs on support (Section IV):** This is an IV / 2SLS approach. Binary beliefs (does not lose; tax is effective) are instrumented by the randomized information treatments. Self-interest is also identified through a fuzzy RDD that uses random eligibility for the targeted dividend around the income-percentile thresholds. Outcomes are approval ("Yes") and acceptance (not "No") of the reform. Headline results: believing one does not lose raises acceptance by more than 50 p.p., and believing the tax is effective raises approval by more than 40 p.p. (in percentage points, not standardized). Progressivity effects are reported as noncausal.
