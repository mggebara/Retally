## Paper Information
Title: Hate Crimes and Gender Imbalances: Fears over Mate
Abstract (first 500 chars): As the number of refugees rises across the world, anti-refugee violence has become a pressing concern. What ex-
plains the incidence and support of such hate crime? We argue that fears among native men that refugees pose a threat in
the competition for female partners are a critical but understudied factor driving hate crime. Employing a comprehensive
data set on the incidence of hate crime across Germany, we first demonstrate that hate crime rises where men face disad-
vantages in local mating 
Methodology Summary: - **Ecological (municipality-level) analysis, estimator and outcome:** Logistic regression of a binary hate-crime indicator on Excess Males. The hate-crime data come from "Mut gegen rechte Gewalt" (Amadeu Antonio Foundation / PRO ASYL) and cover arson attacks on refugee housing, other attacks on refugee housing such as vandalism, and physical attacks on refugees. There are two specifications. (a) "Sum": a cross-section where the unit is the municipality, coded 1 if it had at least one anti-refugee hate crime over 2015–2017 combined and 0 otherwise. (b) "Annual": a panel where the unit is the municipality-year, coded 1 if the municipality had at least one hate crime that year, for 2015, 2016 and 2017. Out of 11,168 municipalities in total.
- **Key regressor:** Excess Males is the number of men divided by the number of women aged 15–44 in each municipality. It is a raw ratio, not standardised: 1 means no imbalance. The headline quantity is the change in predicted probability of at least one hate crime when Excess Males moves from 1.0 to 1.2 (about the 80th percentile), shown in Figure 1 with 95% CIs. The paper also compares effects of moving each variable from its 20th to its 80th percentile (Excess Males vs. unemployment rate and share highly educated).
- **Controls, fixed effects and standard errors (preferred ecological spec, SI Table C.1):** Population, population density, population change, unemployment rate, ratio of male to female unemployment rate, number of refugees (stock, at county level), change in refugee population (flow), percent highly educated, percent manufacturing, change in percent manufacturing, AfD vote share, and violent crime per capita. County fixed effects in both specifications, plus year fixed effects in the annual panel. Standard errors are clustered at the county level.
- **Sample restriction:** Municipalities in the top and bottom 2.5 percentiles of Excess Males are dropped. These are mostly very small municipalities.
- **Robustness checks (ecological):**
  - a linear probability model (SI C.3);
  - a negative binomial model for incident counts (SI C.5);
  - physical attacks only (SI C.4);
  - other age ranges for the sex ratio (SI C.2);
  - East–West interactions (SI C.6);
  - an alternative measure, Male Disadvantage (male unemployment rate divided by female unemployment rate), controlling for overall unemployment (SI Fig. C.8);
  - interactions with county-level refugee inflows;
  - a placebo analysis (SI C.9).
- **Individual-level survey data:** A Respondi online panel in Germany with 4 waves (September 2016 to December 2017), each about N = 3,000 and designed to be nationally representative on age (18+), gender and state.
  - Mate Competition comes from Wave 4, on a 4-point agree/disagree scale (1–4).
  - Figure 2 shows the share agreeing (agree somewhat or strongly) by terciles of municipal Excess Males, with 95% CIs. It is shown for all respondents, men aged 18–44, and men aged 30–40.
  - Distinctiveness checks regress Mate Competition on being a single male and on Women's Role, controlling for 17 other refugee-related attitudes.
- **List experiment:** Run in Wave 2 (also Wave 1 and the pilot) to check for social desirability bias. 50% of the sample was split into control and treatment groups. The control group saw 3 statements unrelated to hate crime; the treatment group saw the same 3 plus "Only Means". The difference in mean counts estimates the share supporting hate crime, which is compared with the direct question. The direct question was asked of the 75% not in the treatment group.
- **Main individual-level specification (Table 1):** OLS.
  - Dependent variable: Only Means ("violence is sometimes the only means…"), on a 1–4 scale.
  - Key regressor: Mate Competition (1–4, unstandardised). The coefficient is the effect of a one-unit change.
  - Columns are built up step by step:
    - (1) Mate Competition only;
    - (2) adds Job Competition (4-point) and Life Satisfaction (0–10);
    - (3) adds sociodemographics: age, gender, education, income, main economic activity, employment status, German citizenship, household size, self-assessed social status, religious affiliation, state of residence;
    - (4) adds national attitudes toward refugees;
    - (5) adds local attitudes toward refugees;
    - (6), the preferred specification, adds contact with refugees, estimated closeness to refugee shelters, estimated number of refugees in town, views about and closeness to the AfD, left-right self-placement, and attitudes toward Muslims.
  - N is about 3,000 (3,019 in columns 1–2, 3,008 in columns 3–6). Conventional OLS standard errors are reported; ordered logit is a robustness check.
  - Figure 4 repeats column (6) with the outcome swapped for Justified, Message, Prevent and Condemn (Condemn reverse-coded), each 1–4.
