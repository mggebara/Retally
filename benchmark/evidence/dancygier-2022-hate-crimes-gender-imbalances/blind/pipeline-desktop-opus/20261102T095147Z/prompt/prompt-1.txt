## Paper Information
Title: Hate Crimes and Gender Imbalances: Fears over Mate
Abstract (first 500 chars): As the number of refugees rises across the world, anti-refugee violence has become a pressing concern. What ex-
plains the incidence and support of such hate crime? We argue that fears among native men that refugees pose a threat in
the competition for female partners are a critical but understudied factor driving hate crime. Employing a comprehensive
data set on the incidence of hate crime across Germany, we first demonstrate that hate crime rises where men face disad-
vantages in local mating 
Methodology Summary: - **Ecological (municipality-level) analysis, estimation method:** Logistic regression of a binary hate-crime indicator on Excess Males, with standard errors clustered at the county level (Figure 1; full results in SI Appendix C.1, Table C.1). There are two specifications. (a) "Sum": one cross-section of municipalities, where the outcome is 1 if the municipality had at least one anti-refugee hate crime in 2015–2017 and 0 otherwise. (b) "Annual": a municipality-year panel, where the outcome is 1 if the municipality had at least one hate crime in that year. Both include county fixed effects, and the panel also includes year fixed effects. Robustness checks: a linear probability model (SI C.3), a negative binomial model for incident counts (SI C.5), physical attacks only (SI C.4), East–West interactions (SI C.6), other age ranges for the sex ratio (SI C.2), and a placebo analysis (SI C.9).
- **Variables, ecological analysis:** The outcome comes from the "Mut gegen rechte Gewalt" data (Amadeu Antonio Foundation / PRO ASYL). It covers arson attacks on refugee housing, other attacks on refugee housing, and physical attacks on refugees, across 11,168 municipalities in 2015, 2016 and 2017. The key regressor is Excess Males: the number of men divided by the number of women aged 15–44 in the municipality. It enters in levels as a ratio (not standardised), and the headline effect is the change in predicted probability when Excess Males moves from 1.0 (balanced) to 1.2 (about the 80th percentile). Controls (footnote 24): population, population density, population change, unemployment rate, ratio of male to female unemployment rate, number of refugees (stock), change in refugee population (flow), percent highly educated, percent manufacturing, change in percent manufacturing, AfD vote share, and violent crime per capita (definitions in SI Table C.11). The alternative mate-competition measure is Male Disadvantage, the male unemployment rate divided by the female unemployment rate (SI Figure C.8).
- **Sample restriction, ecological analysis:** Municipalities with extreme Excess Males values are dropped (top and bottom 2.5 percentiles). These are mostly very small municipalities.
- **Comparing effect sizes:** For each structural variable, the effect of moving it from its 20th to its 80th percentile (e.g., Excess Males vs. the unemployment rate vs. percent highly educated).
- **Survey data:** Online Respondi panel in Germany with 4 waves (September 2016 to December 2017). Each wave has about 3,000 respondents and was designed to be nationally representative on age (18+), gender and state. Mate Competition ("The inflow of refugees makes it more difficult for native men to find female partners") is asked in Wave 4 on a 1–4 scale (strongly disagree to strongly agree). Descriptives in Figure 2: the share agreeing (with 95% CIs) across terciles of municipality Excess Males, for all respondents, men aged 18–44 and men aged 30–40. The authors also check whether being a single male and the Women's Role item predict Mate Competition, controlling for 17 other refugee-related attitudes (SI Figure D.3).
- **List experiment (Wave 2; Wave 1 in SI D.4):** 50% of the sample is split into control and treatment groups. The control group sees 3 statements unrelated to hate crime; the treatment group sees the same 3 plus the Only Means item. Hate-crime support is estimated as the difference in the mean number of statements respondents agree with. The other 50% plus the control group (75% of the sample) are asked Only Means directly, and the two estimates are compared (Figure 3).
- **Individual-level regressions (Table 1):** OLS. The dependent variable is Only Means ("violence is sometimes the only means…", 1–4 scale). The key regressor is Mate Competition (1–4), unstandardised. Columns add controls step by step: (1) Mate Competition only; (2) + Job Competition (4-point scale) and Life Satisfaction (0–10); (3) + sociodemographics (age, gender, education, income, main economic activity, employment status, German citizenship, household size, self-assessed social status, religious affiliation, state of residence); (4) + attitudes toward refugees, national; (5) + attitudes toward refugees, local; (6, preferred) + additional controls: public contact with refugees, closeness to refugee shelters, estimated number of refugees in town, views about and closeness to the AfD, left-right self-placement, and attitudes toward Muslims. The paper reports conventional OLS standard errors; no clustering is mentioned. Sample sizes: 3,019 in columns 1–2 and 3,008 in columns 3–6. Robustness: ordered logit, a men-only subsample (SI D.6), and East–West interactions (SI D.5.2).
- **Other outcomes (Figure 4):** The column (6) specification is re-estimated with the other hate-crime-support outcomes Justified, Message, Prevent and Condemn (Condemn reverse-coded; all 1–4). The reported quantity is the effect of a one-unit change in Mate Competition, with 95% CIs (SI Table D.5.1). There is no train/test split or cross-validation; inference uses standard regression standard errors and confidence intervals.
