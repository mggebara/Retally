## Paper Information
Title: Hate Crimes and Gender Imbalances: Fears over Mate
Abstract (first 500 chars): As the number of refugees rises across the world, anti-refugee violence has become a pressing concern. What ex-
plains the incidence and support of such hate crime? We argue that fears among native men that refugees pose a threat in
the competition for female partners are a critical but understudied factor driving hate crime. Employing a comprehensive
data set on the incidence of hate crime across Germany, we first demonstrate that hate crime rises where men face disad-
vantages in local mating 
Methodology Summary: - **Ecological design (municipality level, Germany, 2015–2017):** Logistic regression of anti-refugee hate crime incidence on *Excess Males* (number of men ÷ number of women aged 15–44 in the municipality; a raw ratio, not standardised). Hate crime data come from "Mut gegen rechte Gewalt" (Amadeu Antonio Foundation & PRO ASYL) and cover arson against refugee housing, other attacks on refugee housing, and physical attacks on refugees. The DV is binary in two versions: (a) **Sum**: 1 if the municipality had ≥1 hate crime at any point in 2015–2017 (17% of municipalities; unit = municipality); (b) **Annual**: 1 if ≥1 hate crime in a given year (unit = municipality-year panel; 5.6%/12.1%/6.8% of 11,168 municipalities in 2015/2016/2017).
- **Preferred ecological specification:** logit with **county and year fixed effects** and controls for population, population density, population change, unemployment rate, ratio of male to female unemployment rate (Male Disadvantage), number of refugees (stock, county level), change in refugee population (flow), % highly educated, % manufacturing, change in % manufacturing, AfD vote share, and violent crime per capita (SI Table C.1/C.11). **SEs clustered at the county level.** Headline quantity: change in predicted probability of ≥1 hate crime when Excess Males goes from 1.0 to 1.2 (≈80th percentile): +2.60 pp (95% CI [0.88, 4.19]) and +1.71 pp (95% CI [0.76, 2.55]) across the Sum/Annual models (Figure 1, right panel).
- **Sample restriction (ecological):** observations with extreme Excess Males values are dropped (top and bottom 2.5 percentiles; mostly very small municipalities, median population 247). No train/test split or cross-validation; this is inferential regression.
- **Ecological robustness checks:** linear probability model (SI C.3); negative binomial on incident counts (SI Table C.5); physical attacks only (SI Table C.4); other age ranges for the sex ratio (SI Table C.2); East–West interactions (SI Table C.6); the alternative mate-competition measure Male Disadvantage = male ÷ female unemployment rate (r = −0.048 with Excess Males; SI Fig. C.8); a placebo analysis (SI C.9); interactions with county refugee inflows (results mixed); 20th→80th percentile effect comparisons with unemployment and education.
- **Individual-level survey data:** Respondi online panel, 4 waves (Sept 2016–Dec 2017), each ≈3,000 respondents, quota-representative on age (18+), gender and Bundesland. *Mate Competition* ("The inflow of refugees makes it more difficult for native men to find female partners", 1–4 scale, from strongly disagree to strongly agree) was asked in Wave 4. Figure 2 shows the proportion agreeing (with 95% CIs and p-values) across terciles of municipal Excess Males, for all respondents, men aged 18–44 and men aged 30–40. Validity checks: single-male status and Women's Role predict Mate Competition, controlling for 17 other refugee-related attitudes (SI Fig. D.3).
- **List experiment (Wave 2, also pilot and Wave 1):** 50% of the sample was split into control (3 non-sensitive items) and treatment (same items plus *Only Means*). Prevalence = difference in mean item counts (2.09 − 1.94 ≈ 15%), compared with direct questioning of the remaining 75% (18%); the difference is not significant, so direct items are used afterwards.
- **Main individual-level regression (Table 1):** OLS of *Only Means* ("violence is sometimes the only means…", 1–4) on *Mate Competition* (1–4; raw scale units, not standardised); ordered logit gives similar results. Columns add terms in sequence: (1) Mate Competition alone (0.437, SE 0.016, N = 3,019); (2) + Job Competition (1–4) and Life Satisfaction (0–10) (0.263); (3) + sociodemographics: age, gender, education, income, main economic activity, employment status, German citizenship, household size, self-assessed social status, religious affiliation, state of residence (0.236, N = 3,008); (4) + national attitudes toward refugees (0.206); (5) + local attitudes toward refugees (0.185); (6, preferred) + additional controls: public contact with refugees, estimated closeness to refugee shelters, estimated number of refugees in town, views about and closeness to the AfD, left-right self-placement, attitudes toward Muslims (0.155, SE 0.019, R² = 0.459, adj. R² = 0.431, N = 3,008). Conventional SEs are reported in parentheses; significance markers are † p<0.1, * p<0.05, ** p<0.01.
- **Other outcomes and evaluation:** Figure 4 re-estimates the column (6) specification for each DV (Only Means, Justified, Message, Prevent, Condemn [reverse-coded]; all 1–4) and reports the effect of a one-unit change in Mate Competition with 95% CIs. Effects are positive and precise for the four goal-oriented items; the Condemn effect is smaller and weaker. Further checks: results hold when the sample is restricted to men (SI D.6) and with East–West interactions (SI Table D.5.2). Evaluation is based on coefficients, CIs/p-values, R²/adjusted R² and predicted-probability changes; there are no predictive accuracy metrics.
