Title: NBER WORKING PAPER SERIES
Abstract (first 500 chars): Can strengthening intellectual property (IP) protection for producers of one good affect 
innovation in other related goods? To answer this question, we exploit a unique policy 
experiment in the interwar military aircraft industry. Airframe designs had little IP protection 
before 1926, but changes passed by Congress in 1926 provided airframe manufacturers with 
enhanced property rights over new designs. We show that granting property rights to air-frame 
producers increased innovation in airfr
Methodology Summary: - **Research design / identification:** A natural policy experiment. Section 10 of the 1926 Air Corps Act let military procurement switch from competitive bidding to negotiated contracts, which in effect gave airframe makers exclusive production rights over new military designs. Engine makers had patent protection throughout, so their IP regime did not change. The paper compares innovation before and after 1926 (i) within the United States over time and (ii) in the US against comparison countries that had no such change: mainly the United Kingdom, plus Japan, Germany and Italy where data allow. This is a difference-in-differences-style comparison of trend breaks. Countries are treated as largely separate military aircraft markets that buy from domestic producers, so the cross-country comparison absorbs common shocks to the world technology frontier, such as widely shared NACA and foreign research.
- **Theory guiding the tests:** A partial-equilibrium model with Leontief complementarity between airframes and engines. The government buys a quantity x_m that falls in P_A + P_E (utility θx^ρ + leftover budget, with ρ ∈ (1/3, 1)). Without IP, airframes are priced by Bertrand competition (P_A = γ_A). With IP, prices come from Nash-in-Nash monopolist–monopsonist bargaining. Testable predictions: IP for airframes raises airframe innovation and lowers engine innovation (the ratio of engine profits with vs. without airframe IP is ((3ρ−1)/(2ρ))^(ρ/(1−ρ)) < 1), and it creates incentives for "vertical-complement" airframe–engine mergers when ρ ∈ (1/3, 1/2).
- **Data and unit of observation:** Hand-compiled from aircraft reference books (sources in Appendix C). The data cover the near-universe of important military airframes, matched to the aero-engines they used, from 1918 to 1935 (end of WWI to the start of WWII mobilisation). One observation is the introduction of a new airframe model, including new versions of existing designs (e.g., PW-9, PW-9A, PW-9B). Each observation records year of first delivery, producer, physical and performance characteristics, and number delivered. Price and profit data are not available.
- **Sample restrictions:** The main analysis covers fighters and bombers only, and the engines used in them. These are the high-performance segments that are most distinct from civil designs. Other segments, such as reconnaissance, appear only in the appendix. Some candidate comparison countries were dropped for data reasons (data appendix).
- **Outcome variables (innovation measured by performance, not by patents or design counts):**
  - Airframes: wing load is preferred, defined as gross weight (lb) / wing area (sq ft). Maximum speed (mph) is a secondary measure. The measures are highly correlated (Appendix Fig. E1).
  - Engines: horsepower / engine weight (lb), which the paper treats as the key measure; horsepower / displacement (litres); and horsepower / frontal area (sq in).
  - Supplementary engine outcomes: the pace of new liquid-cooled engine development, the lag between engine vintage and airframe vintage, and qualitative evidence on mergers from firm histories.
- **Estimation / evaluation:** Main evidence is graphical: performance plotted against year of introduction for the US and the comparison countries (Fig. 1, panels A for wing load and B for max speed). The test is whether the rate of improvement (the slope over time) changes after 1926 in the US relative to the UK and others. The paper allows a 1–2 year design-development lag (Appendix D) when interpreting the timing. The prediction is faster post-1926 improvement for US airframes and slower post-1926 improvement for US engines, especially hp/weight. There is no machine-learning train/test split or cross-validation; this is a historical econometric study.
