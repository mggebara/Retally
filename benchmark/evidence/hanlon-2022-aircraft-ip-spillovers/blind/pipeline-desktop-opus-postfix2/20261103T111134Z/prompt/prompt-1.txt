## Paper Information
Title: NBER WORKING PAPER SERIES
Abstract (first 500 chars): Can strengthening intellectual property (IP) protection for producers of one good affect 
innovation in other related goods? To answer this question, we exploit a unique policy 
experiment in the interwar military aircraft industry. Airframe designs had little IP protection 
before 1926, but changes passed by Congress in 1926 provided airframe manufacturers with 
enhanced property rights over new designs. We show that granting property rights to air-frame 
producers increased innovation in airfr
Methodology Summary: - **Identification / design:** A natural policy experiment. Section 10 of the Air Corps Act of 1926 replaced competitive bidding for military airframe production contracts with negotiated contracts, which in effect gave airframe designers exclusive production rights (IP protection) over new designs. Aero-engine producers had patent protection throughout and saw no change. The analysis is a before/after comparison around 1926 within the United States, plus a difference-in-differences-style comparison of US trends before and after 1926 against comparison countries. The United Kingdom is the main comparison country; Japan, Germany and Italy are used where data exist. The comparison countries are treated as largely isolated domestic military-aircraft markets that had no matching policy change.
- **Theory guiding the tests:** A static partial-equilibrium model with Leontief complementarity between airframes and engines, and government demand x_m = (P_A+P_E)^{1/(ρ−1)}(ρθ)^{1/(1−ρ)} with ρ∈(1/3,1). Without airframe IP, airframe markets are Bertrand. With it, prices come from Nash-in-Nash monopolist–monopsonist bargaining (Horn–Wolinsky; Collard-Wexler et al.). Testable predictions: (i) airframe innovation speeds up after 1926; (ii) engine innovation slows; (iii) airframe and engine makers gain incentives for "vertical-complement" mergers (for ρ∈(1/3,1/2)).
- **Data / sample:** Hand-compiled from books describing airframe and aero-engine designs. Covers the near-universe of important military airframes from 1918–1935 (end of WWI to the start of WWII mobilisation), each matched to the engines it used, for the US and comparison countries. The unit of observation is the introduction of a new airframe model, including new versions of existing designs (e.g., PW-9A, PW-9B), dated by year of first delivery. Recorded fields include producer, physical and performance characteristics, and number delivered. The main sample is restricted to fighters and bombers and their engines. Other segments, such as reconnaissance, are reported in the appendix.
- **Outcome variables (innovation measured directly, not with patents):**
  - **Airframes:** wing load is preferred, defined as gross weight (lb) divided by wing area (sq ft). Maximum speed (mph) is a secondary measure.
  - **Engines:** horsepower per engine weight (lb), horsepower per displacement (litres), and horsepower per frontal area (sq in).
  - **Supplementary outcomes:** the pace of new liquid-cooled engine development, and the gap between an engine's vintage and the vintage of the airframe it is fitted to.
  - **Excluded:** counts of designs, because they may be endogenous and definitions differ across sources. Prices and firm profits, because data are unavailable.
- **Estimation / evaluation:** Graphical and regression comparisons of performance growth rates (trends in the performance measures over years) before and after 1926. These are run for the US alone and for the US relative to the UK and other countries. The post-1926 change in US trend relative to comparison countries is the treatment effect. Engines are the "spillover" market. Merger predictions are evaluated qualitatively using firm histories.
- **Timing considerations:** The authors note that designing a new original airframe took roughly one to two years, which bears on when effects should appear after 1926. They address confounders separately: the 1925 Air Mail (Kelly) Act, the 1926 Air Commerce Act and civil aviation (by focusing on high-performance military segments), NACA research funding, and the Great Depression.
- **Not stated in the available text:** The paper excerpt ends at the start of Section 5.1. So the exact regression equation, functional form (e.g., logs of the performance measures), fixed effects, control set and standard-error clustering are not given here. No ML-style train/test split or cross-validation is involved.
