Methodology Summary: - **Identification / estimation method:** A natural experiment from the 1926 Air Corps Act (Section 10), which in practice replaced competitive bidding with negotiated contracts. This gave airframe makers exclusive production rights over new military designs. Engine makers had patent protection throughout, so nothing changed for them. The paper compares innovation before and after 1926 in two ways: (i) within the United States over time, as a change in the trend or growth rate of performance, and (ii) the US against comparison countries, as a difference-in-differences on the post-1926 change in the rate of improvement. The United Kingdom is the main comparison country; Japan, Germany and Italy are used where data exist. The DiD is effectively US × post-1926 × time trend. The excerpt is cut off before the formal estimating equation, so the exact specification, fixed effects and standard-error treatment are not given in the supplied text.
- **Unit of observation and data:** One observation is the introduction of a new military airframe model, including new versions of an existing design (e.g., PW-9, PW-9A, PW-9B). Each model is matched to the aero-engine it uses. The data cover 1918–1935 and were compiled from books describing airframe and aero-engine designs (sources in Appendix C). Recorded for each model: year of first delivery (the time variable), producer, physical and performance characteristics, and number delivered. Engines are observed by design and vintage.
- **Sample restrictions:** The window runs from 1918 to 1935 (end of WWI to the start of WWII mobilisation). The main analysis keeps only fighters and bombers and the engines used in them, because these are the high-performance segments most distinct from civil designs. Other segments (e.g., reconnaissance) appear only in the appendix. Some candidate comparison countries are excluded for data reasons (data appendix).
- **Airframe outcomes:** The preferred measure is wing load, defined as gross weight (lb) divided by wing area (sq ft). Maximum speed (mph) is an alternative. Other measures are reported for correlation checks (Appendix Fig. E1). Outcomes are performance levels plotted or regressed against year of first delivery, and the object of interest is the rate of improvement (slope) before and after 1926. The supplied text does not say whether outcomes are in logs. It never describes them as standardised, so treat them as in natural units.
- **Engine outcomes:** Three performance ratios: horsepower per pound of engine weight (the key one), horsepower per litre of displacement, and horsepower per square inch of frontal area. There are also qualitative or count-based measures:
  - the pace of new liquid-cooled (high-performance) engine designs;
  - the vintage gap, meaning engine vintage minus the vintage of the airframe it powers, compared between the US and other countries before and after 1926.
- **Supplementary tests:** Firm histories are used to look for vertical-complement mergers between airframe and engine makers after 1926 (e.g., 1929), as predicted by the Nash-in-Nash bargaining model when ρ ∈ (1/3, 1/2). The paper also discusses alternative explanations: the Kelly Air Mail Act (1925), the Air Commerce Act (1926), NACA funding trends (Appendix Fig. B3), the civil market, and the Depression. Appendix D documents design lead times of 1–2 years.
- **Evaluation:** This is not a predictive or ML exercise. There is no train/test split, cross-validation or hyperparameters. Inference rests on graphical evidence (Figure 1, Panels A–B for fighters and bombers) and regression comparisons of how fast performance improved before and after 1926, in the US against the comparison countries. The theory model's parameter ρ is not estimated.
