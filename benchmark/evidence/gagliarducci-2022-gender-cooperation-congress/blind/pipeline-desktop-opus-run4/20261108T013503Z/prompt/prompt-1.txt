Methodology Summary: - **Data and unit of analysis:** Each observation is one House public bill (simple, concurrent and joint resolutions and private bills are excluded) from the 101st to the 111th Congress (elected 1988 to 2008), taken from THOMAS and cross-checked against Adler and Wilkerson's Congressional Bills Project and the Fowler et al. cosponsorship data. These are linked to sponsor biographical data (ICPSR up to the 104th Congress, then the Biographical Directory), House Clerk election returns, and Census district demographics. Results are reported for all members pooled and separately for Democrats and Republicans.
- **Dependent variables (two cooperativeness measures):** (a) the total number of cosponsors on the bill (coalition building), and (b) the percent of cosponsors from the opposite party, where the denominator is the number of cosponsors plus 1 (the sponsor) so that bills with zero cosponsors are kept (bipartisanship). Robustness checks divide by cosponsors only (zero-cosponsor bills become missing), or use the count of opposite-party cosponsors with the number of cosponsors as a regressor. Both outcomes are in levels, not logs, and not standardised.
- **Baseline OLS specification (eq. 4, Table 3):** Y_ijkt = α + β·Female_i + Congress (time) fixed effects + bill controls + sponsor controls + district controls + ε. Controls are added step by step:
  - (1) Congress fixed effects only.
  - (2) adds bill-category dummies: 226 mutually exclusive minor topics (congressionalbills.org classification) and 33 committees of referral.
  - (3) adds individual controls: tenure, a rookie dummy, age, a committee chair/ranking member dummy, an Ivy League dummy, five previous-occupation dummies, and the total number of bills the member sponsored.
  - (4) adds district controls instead of individual ones: three macro-region dummies, % black, % urban, % foreign-born, % over 65, log median income, and log population density.
  - (5) the preferred specification, with all bill, individual and district controls.
  
  District fixed effects were tried and dropped because they gave imprecise estimates.
- **Regression discontinuity (eq. 5):** The sample is restricted to representatives elected in mixed-gender (male vs. female) races. The specification is Y = α + β·Female + Female·f(MV) + f(MV) + Congress fixed effects + ε, where MV is the female candidate's margin of victory over the male candidate and f(MV) is allowed to differ on each side of the zero threshold. It is run pooled and by party. Validity is checked with McCrary density tests of the running variable, pooled and by party (Figure 6). Within-party RD needs stronger assumptions because the races on each side of the threshold may come from different pools.
- **Matching:** Matching estimators are used as a further way to address women's selection into particular districts. The details of this part of the method are not in the provided text.
- **Auxiliary analyses:**
  - Table 1 tests the model's assumptions with a regression of the probability that a bill passes the House on: the number of cosponsors, the % of opposite-party cosponsors, and their interaction. Column 2 interacts these terms with a female-sponsor dummy and runs a joint test that the three interactions are zero.
  - The predicted Republican vote share comes from an OLS regression of the actual Republican vote share on the district controls listed above (Figure 1).
  - Figures show the DW-NOMINATE first dimension and the Party Unity Score (the share of divisive roll calls in which the member votes with the party) by gender and party.
  - Heterogeneity analyses cover the gender of cosponsors, sponsor seniority, and bills on women's issues.
- **Inference:** The provided text does not say how standard errors are computed or clustered. The paper uses conventional significance levels and joint F/p-value tests.
