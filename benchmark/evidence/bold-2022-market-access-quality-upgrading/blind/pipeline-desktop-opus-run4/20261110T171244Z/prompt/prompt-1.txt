Methodology Summary: - **Design / sample frames:** Four field experiments with smallholder maize farmers in western Uganda (Kakumiro and Kibaale districts), drawn from three sample frames: frame 1 = 20 randomly selected communities in Kakumiro, each at least 5 km apart (market access + extension trial); frame 2 = 20 communities in Kibaale, also at least 5 km apart (extension-only trial); frame 3 = 20 villages randomly drawn from those adjacent to frame-1 control villages (quality measurement, returns-to-quality experiment, and a quasi-control group for counterfactual quality). In each village a census identified smallholder households (maize gardens of no more than 5 acres) that grew maize in the previous season, and about 10 farmers per village were sampled.
- **Quality measurement (Experiment 1):** Enumerators visually inspected every bag offered for sale for 10 defect types (dirty, cobs, stones, dust, insects; broken, immature, damaged, rotten, moldy grain), giving "visually verifiable defects" (count out of 10). They bought one randomly selected bag per farmer for lab testing in Kampala: a 200 g sample, with "lab verified defects" = share of the sample's weight that is non-grain or defective. Moisture was measured with a mobile meter, and "wet maize" = 1 if moisture is above 13%. Aflatoxin was tested with AflaCheck (VICAM) strips at the 10 ppb UNBS cutoff. Lab samples were graded under the East African Standard (grades 1–3 vs. ungraded). Samples: 50 control households from a 100-farmer, 9-village frame-3 sample (one season; visual and lab tests), plus 30 households from 6 more frame-3 villages over two seasons (lab and aflatoxin tests); 44 bags were tested in the lab. The link between visual and lab quality is estimated by local polynomial regression (Fig. 1A). The probability that aflatoxin exceeds 10 ppb is modelled as a function of lab defects on a log scale (online App. G Table 2, spec. 3).
- **Returns-to-quality experiment (Experiment 2):** Individual-level randomization of 99 consenting households (out of 100 approached) into treatment (49) and control (50). Treatment was a free harvest and post-harvest service package (drying, winnowing, sorting, using portable machinery). Households were paid UGX10,000 (treatment) or UGX30,000 (control) for contacting the team before selling. Balance was checked on pre-harvest covariates with a joint balance test (App. G Table 3). The data are 622 inspected bags and 116 sales from 94 households (1 farmer did not sell and 4 could not be reached).
- **Returns-to-quality estimating equations (Table 2):** OLS of the outcome on a treatment indicator. Outcomes: (1) visual defects, with the bag as the unit and village FE; (2) lab defects, measured as grams of defects per 200 g sample on one random bag per household, with village FE; (3) price in UGX/kg, (4) deductions = (y − z)/y in %, where y is the enumerator-measured weight and z is the agreed weight, and (5) net price = pz/y. Columns 3–5 use the sale as the unit, with village FE and week-of-sale FE. Price, deductions and net price are in levels (UGX/kg or %), not logs or standardised units. Standard errors are robust in column 2 and clustered by household in columns 1 and 3–5. A nonparametric Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compares the treatment and control CDFs of price (net of village and week FE, bottom 1% capped in the figure) and of deductions.
- **Market access experiment (Experiment 3):** Clustered repeated-measurement design with 20 village clusters from frame 1, randomly assigned 12 to treatment (buying) and 8 to control, and surveyed over 7 seasons: 3 baseline seasons, then the intervention from the end of season 3 for 4 consecutive seasons. A vertically integrated agro-trading company bought maize that passed visual and moisture checks at a fixed price set with a premium (about 5% over trading-centre prices, about 15% over village prices). The premium comes from a model where the farmer is indifferent between selling high and low quality maize: the 14 pp share of defects and waste is valued at the premium price, and local prices are assumed to be 10% below trading-centre prices. Agents did not bargain or make deductions. The treatment was bundled with extension services: a demo garden and 5 hands-on training meetings over the first two seasons.
- **Extension-only experiment (Experiment 4):** Same design in frame 2, but with 18 clusters (2 dropped after a land conflict) followed over 6 seasons. The clusters were randomly split into two equal groups, and the intervention began at the end of season 3 and ran for 3 seasons.
- **Data and preprocessing for the market and extension trials:** Household surveys at the end of each selling season collected the components of the profit function: harvest, quantity sold, price and revenue for each sale, input and chemical expenses, seed varieties, pre- and post-harvest practices, and hours and expenses for hired and family labour. Maize plot areas were measured with GPS, and from the second season farmers kept recall forms. Outliers are kept in the core sample. As a robustness check, effects are also estimated after trimming the top 1% of observations (and the bottom 1% as well for profit).
- **Quality outcomes in the market trial:** Quality was measured only for sales to the company in treatment (randomly selected bags were lab-graded). Counterfactual quality comes from a quasi-control group: maize buying organised in a random subset of frame-3 villages next to the control villages. Because not all treatment farmers sold to the company, missing quality data in the treatment group are treated as nonrandom one-sided attrition, which yields a lower bound on the causal effect on quality (the validity check is in App. G Table 9). Spillovers to farmers selling to local traders are studied with a difference-in-differences adjustment for selection.
