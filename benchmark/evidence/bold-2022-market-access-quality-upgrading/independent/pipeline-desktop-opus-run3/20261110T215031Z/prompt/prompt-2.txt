Title: American Economic Review 2022, 112(8): 2518–2552
Methodology: - **Design / estimation method:** Four field experiments (RCTs) with smallholder maize farmers in Kakumiro and Kibaale districts, western Uganda, estimated by OLS treatment–control comparisons; no ML, no train/test split or cross-validation. Sample frames: (1) 20 randomly selected villages in Kakumiro, at least 5 km apart, used for the market access + extension trial (12 treatment "buying" clusters, 8 control; 7 seasons, 3 baseline + 4 post-treatment); (2) 20 villages in Kibaale for the extension-only trial (randomized into two equal groups; 2 villages dropped because of a land conflict, leaving 18 clusters over 6 seasons, 3 baseline + 3 treatment); (3) 20 villages adjacent to the frame-1 control villages, used for the quality-measurement and returns-to-quality experiments and as a quasi-control group for counterfactual quality. About 10 smallholder households per village, taken from a census (maize garden ≤ 5 acres, grew maize the previous season).
- **Quality measurement (Section III, Table 1, Figure 1):** "Visually verifiable defects" is the number of 10 defect types (dirt, cobs, stones, dust, insects, broken, immature, damaged, rotten, moldy) that enumerators found in each bag. "Lab verified defects" is the % by weight of non-grain matter plus defective grain in a 200 g sample from one randomly bought bag per farmer. "Wet maize" = 1 if moisture is above 13% (mobile meter). Aflatoxin is a binary indicator for exceeding the 10 ppb UNBS limit (AflaCheck kit). Bags are graded under the East African Standard (EAS) as grade 1/2/3 or ungraded. Figure 1A is a local polynomial regression of lab defects (log scale) on visual defects. Figure 1B gives the predicted probability that aflatoxin exceeds 10 ppb as a function of log lab defects (Appendix G Table 2, spec 3). The unit of observation is a bag.
- **Returns-to-quality experiment (Section IV, Table 2):** 99 consenting households, individually randomized: 49 treatment, offered a free harvest/post-harvest service package (drying, winnowing, sorting), and 50 control. Balance is checked on pre-harvest covariates, including a joint test (Appendix G Table 3). Each outcome is regressed on a treatment dummy:
  - Col 1: visual defects, bag level, N=622, village FE.
  - Col 2: lab defects as a share, N=82, village FE.
  - Cols 3–5: price (UGX/kg), deductions = (y−z)/y in %, and net price = pz/y. Sale level, N=116 sales from 94 households, village FE plus week-of-sale FE.
  
  Outcomes are in levels and not standardized. Standard errors are clustered by household in cols 1 and 3–5 and heteroskedasticity-robust in col 2. Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests compare the treatment and control CDFs of price (D=0.114, p=0.844; price conditional on village and week FE, bottom 1% capped in the graph) and of deductions (D=0.168, p=0.388). Headline: price effect 2.95 (SE 9.87, p=0.766) against a control mean of 731.25 UGX/kg, with a 95% CI of −2.2% to +3.1%.
- **Market-access intervention:** A vertically integrated agro-trading company bought maize in treatment villages that passed visual inspection with no visible defects and moisture below 13%. It paid a fixed price set at a premium of about 5% over trading-center prices, roughly 15% over village prices, with no bargaining or deductions. The premium came from a model (Appendix D): the 14 pp reduction in waste and defects relative to average quality, valued at the premium price, assuming local prices are 10% below trading-center prices. The intervention was combined with extension: a demo garden and 5 hands-on training meetings over the first two seasons, attended by about 70% of invitees in the market trial and 78% in the extension-only trial.
- **Outcome data (market and extension trials):** Household surveys at the end of each selling season. Maize plot areas are measured by GPS, with preloaded GPS data and satellite photos used to confirm plot size. From season 2 onward, recall forms record inputs, labor, and sales. Outcomes are the components of maize profit: harvest, quantity sold, price and revenue (each sale recorded separately), expenses (chemicals, seeds, practices), and hired and family labor costs and hours. The paper also discusses yield, output per hour worked, and profit.
- **Preprocessing and sample restrictions:** Outliers are kept in the core sample after being rechecked for coding errors. As a robustness check, the top 1% of observations is trimmed (for profit, both the top and bottom 1%). In the returns-to-quality experiment, 1 farmer did not sell and 4 could not be reached, leaving 94 selling households. The extension trial drops 2 conflict villages.
- **Quality effect in the market trial:** Quality is measured only on sales to the company in treatment villages (random bags lab-tested) and in a random subset of quasi-control villages in sample frame 3. Missing quality data for treatment farmers who did not sell to the company are handled as non-random one-sided attrition, which gives a lower bound on the causal effect (validity check in Appendix G Table 9).
- **Further evaluation:** Spillovers to local-trader prices within treatment villages are estimated with a difference-in-difference approach that adjusts for selection; local prices account for about 30% of the average price increase. find_col <- function(df, patterns, exclude = NULL) {
  nm <- names(df)
  for (p in patterns) {
    hit <- grep(p, nm, value = TRUE, ignore.case = TRUE)
    if (!is.null(exclude)) hit <- hit[!grepl(exclude, hit, ignore.case = TRUE)]
    if (length(hit) > 0) return(hit[1])
  }
  NULL
}
