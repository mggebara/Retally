## Paper Information
Title: American Economic Journal: Applied Economics 2022, 14(3): 445–487
Abstract (first 500 chars): 445 Assortative Matching at the Top of the Distribution: Evidence from the World’s Most Exclusive Marriage Market† By Marc Goñi* Using novel data on peerage marriages in Britain, I find that low
Methodology Summary: - **Design / identification:** Quasi-experiment based on the interruption of the London Season in 1861–1863 (Queen Victoria's mourning). Because the decision to marry during the interruption is endogenous, identification comes from comparing female cohorts with different age-based risks of marrying in 1861–1863. No train/test split or cross-validation (econometric paper).
- **Treatment variable (synthetic probability, eq. 1):** T_t = p(t) + p(t+1) + p(t+2), where t ∈ {15,…,35} is the woman's age in 1861 and p(t) is the percentage of women marrying at age t in "normal times". p(t) is computed from a benchmark cohort of peers' daughters born 1815–1830, excluding those who married after 1861 or died before age 30. T is measured in percent, and the paper evaluates effects per one-SD (7.3 pp) change. Robustness checks use alternative benchmark cohorts (Section IIIE). An IV variant instruments a woman's actual marriage during 1861–1863 with T_t.
- **Data and preprocessing:** Hollingsworth (2001) peerage genealogies (~26,000 individuals), linked to newly digitised Bateman (1883) family landholdings (owners of ≥3,000 acres plus 1,300 owners of ≥2,000 acres; acreage of birth families) and to geo-referenced family seats from Burke (1826; 694 seats, 498 families). Titles are coded in 5 categories: duke/marquess/earl; baron/viscount; baronet; knight; commoner. Landholdings are converted to rank percentiles in acres. Distance from family seat to London is computed from the seats.
- **Estimation samples:** The baseline is all peers' daughters aged 15–35 in 1861 who ever married (N=644), excluding second marriages, women married to foreigners, and royals. The landholding-sorting outcomes (cols 3–5) use women for whom Bateman lists both spouses' family landholdings (N=324). The never-married outcome (col 6) uses women aged 15–35 in 1861 including never-married women, excluding those who died before age 35 (N=765). The Panel B distance-to-London specification is restricted to women with a recorded family seat (N=484 / 260 / 565).
- **Estimating equations:** For binary outcomes (married a commoner, married an heir, married down, never married), a probit (eq. 2): Pr(y_it=1|X) = Φ(α + βT_t + X′δ). For continuous outcomes, OLS (eq. 3): Y_it = A + B·T_t + X′Δ + ε. Y is the difference between spouses' family-landholding rank percentiles, either in absolute value (col 3; larger values mean less sorting) or as husband − wife (col 4). Coefficients are in raw units (outcome per 1 pp of synthetic probability); nothing is stated to be standardised.
- **Controls (Table 2):** Indicators for duke/marquess/earl daughter and for English peerage, plus birth order excluding heirs. Panel B adds the distance from family seat to London. There are no fixed effects.
- **Inference:** Standard errors are clustered by birth year (G = 21 clusters). Bootstrap-t p-values (Cameron, Gelbach and Miller 2008) are also reported: wild bootstrap for the OLS models and pairs cluster resampling for the probits. Probit fit is reported as "percent correctly predicted".
- **Additional checks:** Placebo tests re-define the sample and treatment x ∈ {10,…,50} years earlier (fake interruptions in 1861−x to 1863−x) for the married-a-commoner outcome. Nonparametric checks use chi-squared tests of association for sorting by title and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests for landholdings. Balance checks compare marriages in 1858–1860, 1861–1863 and 1864–1866 (Table 1). Later sections estimate IV models linking marriage to a commoner (instrumented by T) to brothers' MP election outcomes (27 general elections and 97 by-elections, 1776–1910; English seats) and to school-board wealth taxes (943 boards within a 10-mile radius of 387 family seats, 1872–1878).
